Photosynthesis: The process by which green plants use sunlight to synthesize foods from carbon dioxide and water, involving the green pigment chlorophyll and generating oxygen as a byproduct. 
 Transpiration: The exhalation of water vapor through the stomata of a plant's leaves, acting as a "pump" to pull water and nutrients from the roots to the canopy. 
 Photoperiodism: The physiological reaction of organisms to the length of night or a dark period, which determines the timing of flowering in short-day, long-day, and day-neutral plants. 
 Vernalization: The cooling of seed or plant during germination or growth in order to accelerate flowering, essential for winter cereals. 
 Emasculation: The removal of the anthers of a flower before they have shed pollen to prevent self-pollination in plant breeding. 
 Heterosis (Hybrid Vigor): The tendency of a crossbred individual to show qualities superior to those of both parents. 
 Allellopathy: The chemical inhibition of one plant (or other organism) by another, due to the release into the environment of substances acting as germination or growth inhibitors. 
 Guttation: The secretion of droplets of water from the pores of plants (hydathodes), typically due to high root pressure at night when transpiration is low. 
 Dormancy: A period in an organism's life cycle when growth, development, and physical activity are temporarily stopped to survive adverse environmental conditions. 
 Apomixis: Asexual reproduction in plants, specifically the production of seeds without fertilization, creating clones of the mother plant. 
 Parthenocarpy: The development of a fruit without prior fertilization, which typically results in a seedless fruit. 
 C3 Plants: Plants that use the Calvin cycle for the initial steps that incorporate CO2 into organic matter, producing a three-carbon compound. 
 C4 Plants: Plants that preface the Calvin cycle with reactions that incorporate CO2 into four-carbon compounds, highly efficient in hot, dry climates. 
 CAM Plants: Plants that use Crassulacean Acid Metabolism, opening stomata at night to fix CO2 and closing them during the day to minimize water loss. 
 Turgor Pressure: The force within the cell that pushes the plasma membrane against the cell wall, maintained by water intake. 
 Etiolation: A process in flowering plants grown in partial or complete absence of light, characterized by long, weak stems and pale color. 
 Senescence: The condition or process of deterioration with age in plant tissues, often leading to leaf fall or plant death. 
 Abscission: The natural detachment of parts of a plant, typically dead leaves and ripe fruit. 
 Inflorescence: The complete flower head of a plant including stems, stalks, bracts, and flowers. 
 Monoecious: Having both male and female reproductive organs on the same individual plant. 
 Dioecious: Having the male and female reproductive organs on separate individual plants. 
Soil Texture: The relative proportion of sand, silt, and clay particles in a soil mass. 
 Soil Structure: The arrangement of primary soil particles into compound particles or aggregates (peds). 
 Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC): A measure of how many cations can be retained on soil particle surfaces; an indicator of a soil's ability to hold nutrients. 
 Soil pH: A measure of the acidity or alkalinity of a soil, affecting nutrient availability and microbial activity. 
 Humus: The dark, organic-rich material in soil that is formed by the decomposition of plant and animal matter. 
 Soil Horizon: A layer parallel to the soil surface whose physical, chemical, and biological characteristics differ from the layers above and beneath. 
 Bulk Density: The mass of oven-dry soil per unit volume, including both solids and pore space. 
 Field Capacity: The amount of soil moisture or water content held in the soil after excess water has drained away by gravity. 
 Permanent Wilting Point: The minimum point of soil moisture the plant requires not to wilt. 
 Leaching: The loss of water-soluble plant nutrients from the soil, due to rain and irrigation. 
 Nitrification: The biological oxidation of ammonia or ammonium to nitrite followed by the oxidation of the nitrite to nitrate. 
 Denitrification: The loss or removal of nitrogen or nitrogen compounds; specifically, the reduction of nitrates or nitrites by bacteria in soil that usually results in the escape of nitrogen into the air. 
 Nitrogen Fixation: The chemical processes by which atmospheric nitrogen is assimilated into organic compounds, especially by certain microorganisms. 
 Salinization: The process by which water-soluble salts accumulate in the soil, hindering plant growth. 
 Sodicity: A measure of the amount of available sodium in the soil; high levels cause clay particles to disperse and ruin soil structure. 
 Flocculation: The process by which individual particles of clay aggregate into clot-like masses or precipitate into small lumps. 
 Capillary Water: Water that remains in the soil after gravitational water is drained out, held by surface tension in small pores. 
 Hygroscopic Water: Water absorbed from the atmosphere and held very tightly by the soil particles, unavailable to plants. 
 Eutrophication: Excessive richness of nutrients in a lake or other body of water, frequently due to runoff from the land, which causes a dense growth of plant life and death of animal life from lack of oxygen. 
 Base Saturation: The extent to which the adsorption complex of a soil is occupied by basic cations (Ca, Mg, K, Na). 
Ruminant: An even-toed ungulate mammal that chews the cud regurgitated from its rumen, such as cattle, sheep, and goats. 
 Rumen: The first and largest stomach compartment of a ruminant, serving as a primary site for microbial fermentation of ingested feed. 
 Reticulum: The second compartment of the ruminant stomach, often called the "honeycomb," which traps foreign objects. 
 Omasum: The third compartment of the ruminant stomach, responsible for water and electrolyte absorption. 
 Abomasum: The fourth compartment of the ruminant stomach, known as the "true stomach," where enzymatic digestion occurs. 
 Colostrum: The first secretion from the mammary glands after giving birth, providing the newborn with essential antibodies and nutrients. 
 Gestation: The process or period of being carried in the womb between conception and birth. 
 Lactation: The secretion of milk by the mammary glands. 
 Silage: Grass or other green fodder compacted and stored in airtight conditions, typically in a silo, used as animal feed in the winter or dry season. 
 Roughage: Fibrous indigestion material in vegetable foodstuffs (like hay) which aids the passage of food and waste through the gut of livestock. 
 Concentrate: Animal feed that is rich in energy or protein but low in fiber, such as grains or soybean meal. 
 Zoonosis: A disease which can be transmitted to humans from animals. 
 Inbreeding Depression: The reduced biological fitness in a given population as a result of inbreeding. 
 Artificial Insemination: The medical or genetic procedure of injecting semen into the vagina or uterus. 
 Culling: The reduction of a wild or domestic animal population by selective slaughter to improve herd health or productivity. 
 Dry Period: The period in a dairy cow's lactation cycle when she is not being milked, usually for 60 days prior to calving. 
 FCR (Feed Conversion Ratio): A measure of an animal's efficiency in converting feed mass into increased body mass. 
 Dressing Percentage: The percentage of the live animal's weight that ends up as a carcass. 
 Parturition: The action or process of giving birth to offspring. 
 Estrous Cycle: The recurring reproductive cycle in many female mammals, including heat (estrus). 
Tillage: The preparation of land for growing crops by mechanical manipulation like plowing or harrowing. 
 Zero Tillage (No-Till): A way of growing crops or pasture from year to year without disturbing the soil through tillage. 
 Precision Agriculture: A farming management concept based on observing, measuring, and responding to inter and intra-field variability in crops. 
 Drip Irrigation: A type of micro-irrigation system that has the potential to save water and nutrients by allowing water to drip slowly to the roots of plants. 
 PTO (Power Take-Off): A splined drive shaft on a tractor used to provide power to an attachment or separate machine. 
 Draft: The force required to pull an agricultural implement in the direction of travel. 
 Hydraulic System: A drive or control system that uses pressurized hydraulic fluid to power machinery. 
 Calibration: The process of adjusting a machine (like a sprayer) to ensure it applies the correct amount of material over a given area. 
 Combined Harvester: A versatile machine designed to efficiently harvest a variety of grain crops by reaping, threshing, and winnowing in one process. 
 Contour Bunding: The construction of small earth embankments across a slope to intercept runoff and prevent soil erosion. 
 Terracing: A piece of sloped plane that has been cut into a series of successively receding flat surfaces or platforms, which resemble steps, for the purposes of more effective farming. 
 Subsoiling: Deep tillage used to break up hardpans or compacted subsoil layers without turning the soil over. 
 Post-Harvest Technology: The inter-disciplinary "Science and Technique" applied to agricultural produce after harvest for its protection, conservation, processing, packaging, distribution, and marketing. 
 Controlled Atmosphere Storage: A storage technique where the levels of oxygen, carbon dioxide, and nitrogen are regulated to slow down the ripening and spoilage of fruit. 
 Vermiculture: The cultivation of earthworms, especially in order to use them to convert organic waste into fertilizer (vermicompost). 
Subsistence Farming: Self-sufficiency farming in which the farmers focus on growing enough food to feed themselves and their families. 
 Cash Crop: An agricultural crop which is grown to sell for profit rather than use by the farmer. 
 Opportunity Cost: The loss of potential gain from other alternatives when one alternative is chosen. 
 Law of Diminishing Returns: A principle of stating that if one input in the production of a commodity is increased while all other inputs are held fixed, a point will eventually be reached at which additions of the input yield progressively smaller increases in output. 
 Elasticity of Demand: A measure of how much the quantity demanded of a good responds to a change in the price of that good. 
 Market Equilibrium: A situation in which the quantity of a product demanded equals the quantity supplied, and the price remains stable. 
 Extension Education: An informal educational process directed toward the rural population to provide advice and information on improving farming and home life. 
 Value Chain: The process or activities by which a company or industry adds value to an article, including production, marketing, and the provision after-sales service. 
 Price Floor: A government- or group-imposed price control or limit on how low a price can be charged for a product, commodity, or service. 
 Diffusion of Innovation: A theory that seeks to explain how, why, and at what rate new ideas and technology spread through cultures. 
 Economies of Scale: The cost advantages that enterprises obtain due to their scale of operation, with cost per unit of output decreasing which increasing scale. 
 Diversification: The process of a farm system becoming more varied, growing a range of different crops or keeping different types of livestock to reduce risk. 
Vector: An organism, typically a biting insect or tick, that transmits a disease or parasite from one animal or plant to another. 
 Pathogen: A bacterium, virus, or other microorganism that can cause disease. 
 Fungicide: A chemical that destroys fungus. 
 Systemic Pesticide: A pesticide that is absorbed into a plant and distributed throughout its tissues, reaching parts not directly sprayed. 
 Biological Control: The control of a pest by the introduction of a natural enemy or predator. 
 Integrated Pest Management (IPM): An ecosystem-based strategy that focuses on long-term prevention of pests or their damage through a combination of techniques such as biological control, habitat manipulation, modification of cultural practices, and use of resistant varieties. 
 Chlorosis: Abnormal reduction or loss of the normal green coloration of leaves of plants, typically as a result of iron or nitrogen deficiency. 
 Necrosis: The death of most or all of the cells in an organ or tissue due to disease, injury, or failure of the blood supply. 
 Inoculation: The act of introducing a pathogen or antigen into a living organism to stimulate the production of antibodies or to study the infection. 
 Wilt: A disease caused by the loss of turgor in a plant, making it limp and drooped, usually due to a vascular pathogen blocking water transport. 
 Late Blight: A devastating plant disease caused by the oomycete Phytophthora infestans, which triggered the Irish Potato Famine. 
 Quarantine: A state, period, or place of isolation in which plants or animals that have arrived from elsewhere or been exposed to infectious or contagious disease are placed. 
Agroecology: The study of ecological processes applied to agricultural production systems. 
 Cover Crop: A crop grown for the protection and enrichment of the soil rather than for harvest. 
 Green Manure: A fertilizer consisting of growing plants that are plowed back into the soil. 
 Agroforestry: Agriculture incorporating the cultivation and conservation of trees. 
 Biodiversity: The variety of life in the world or in a particular habitat or ecosystem. 
 Resilience: The capacity of an ecosystem to respond to a perturbation or disturbance by resisting damage and recovering quickly. 
 Carbon Sequestration: A natural or artificial process by which carbon dioxide is removed from the atmosphere and held in solid or liquid form, primarily in soil and forests. 
 Organic Farming: A method of crop and livestock production that involves much more than choosing not to use pesticides, fertilizers, genetically modified organisms, antibiotics, and growth hormones. 
 Monoculture: The cultivation of a single crop in a given area. 
 Polyculture: The simultaneous cultivation or exploitation of several crops or kinds of animals in the same space. 
Marker-Assisted Selection (MAS): A process whereby a marker (morphological, biochemical, or one based on DNA/RNA variation) is used for indirect selection of a genetic determinant or determinants of a trait of interest. 
 Genetically Modified Organism (GMO): Any organism whose genetic material has been altered using genetic engineering techniques. 
 Double Haploid (DH): A genotype formed when haploid cells undergo chromosome doubling, resulting in a completely homozygous line. 
 Phenotype: The set of observable characteristics of an individual resulting from the interaction of its genotype with the environment. 
 Genotype: The genetic constitution of an individual organism. 
 Evapotranspiration: The process by which water is transferred from the land to the atmosphere by evaporation from the soil and other surfaces and by transpiration from plants. 
 Growing Degree Days (GDD): A measure of heat accumulation used by horticulturists and gardeners to predict plant and animal development rates such as the date that a flower will bloom or a crop will reach maturity. 
 Site Index: A species-specific measure of forest site quality, based on the height of dominant and co-dominant trees at a specific reference age. 
 Adsorption: The adhesion of atoms, ions, or molecules from a gas, liquid, or dissolved solid to a surface. 
 Chelation: A type of bonding of ions and molecules to metal ions, used in specialty fertilizers to keep micronutrients available in the soil.Soil Colloids: The most active portion of the soil, consisting of organic and inorganic particles with extremely small diameters (less than 0.001 mm) and carrying negative surface charges. 
 Anion Exchange Capacity (AEC): The sum total of exchangeable anions that a soil can adsorb, which typically increases as soil pH decreases. 
 Particle Density: The weight per unit volume of solid soil particles, excluding pore space; for most mineral soils, it is approximately 2.65 g/cm³. 
 Soil Porosity: The percentage of the total soil volume occupied by pores, which determines the soil's capacity to hold air and water. 
 Soil Consistency: The resistance of a soil to deformation or rupture, described by terms like friable, plastic, or hard, depending on moisture content. 
 Illuviation: The process of deposition of soil material (like clay or iron) removed from one horizon to another in the soil profile, typically into the B horizon. 
 Eluviation: The transport of soil material from upper layers of soil to lower levels by downward precipitation of water across soil horizons. 
 Capillary Potential: The amount of work that must be done per unit quantity of pure water to overcome the attraction of the soil matrix for water. 
 Soil Air: The gaseous phase of the soil, characterized by higher carbon dioxide and lower oxygen concentrations than the atmospheric air due to root and microbial respiration. 
 Clay Minerals: Crystalline substances consisting of sheets of silica and alumina, categorized into 1:1 types (Kaolinite) and 2:1 types (Montmorillonite). 
Duty of Water: The relationship between the volume of water and the area of crop it can mature; it represents the number of hectares irrigated by one cubic meter per second (cumec) of constant flow. 
 Delta: The total depth of water required by a crop during its entire stay on the field, usually expressed in centimeters or meters. 
 Base Period: The time interval between the first watering of a crop at the time of sowing to its last watering before harvesting. 
 Consumptive Use (CU): The total amount of water used by the plant for transpiration and metabolic activities plus the water evaporated from the adjacent soil. 
 Water Requirement: The quantity of water, regardless of its source, required by a crop or diversified pattern of crops in a given period of time for its normal growth under field conditions. 
 Irrigation Requirement: The total amount of water that must be applied to a field through irrigation to meet the crop's water needs, calculated as water requirement minus effective rainfall. 
 Aquifer: A body of permeable rock or sediment that can contain or transmit groundwater. 
 Check Basin Irrigation: A method where the field is divided into small, level plots surrounded by low bunds, and water is applied to each basin to a desired depth. 
 Surface Tension: The property of the surface of a liquid that allows it to resist an external force, critical for capillary water movement in soil. 
 Infiltration: The process by which water on the ground surface enters the soil. 
Polyploidy: The condition in which a normally diploid cell or organism acquires one or more additional sets of chromosomes, common in crops like wheat and sugarcane. 
 Inbreeding: The mating of individuals that are more closely related than the average of the population, often used to create pure lines. 
 Hybridization: The process of crossing two genetically different individuals to combine desirable traits in the offspring. 
 Backcross Method: A form of recurrent crossing with one of the parents to transfer a specific trait (like disease resistance) into an elite variety. 
 Pedigree Method: A system of breeding where individual plants are selected in the F2 and subsequent generations, and their progenies are grown separately with records kept of parent-offspring relationships. 
 Bulk Method: A method of handling segregating generations in which the F2 and subsequent generations are grown in bulk without selection until a high level of homozygosity is reached. 
 Mutation Breeding: The process of exposing seeds to chemicals or radiation to generate mutants with desirable traits to be used in breeding programs. 
 Male Sterility: The failure of a plant to produce functional pollen, which simplifies the production of hybrid seeds by eliminating the need for hand emasculation. 
 Clone: A group of genetically identical cells or organisms derived from a single ancestor through asexual or vegetative propagation. 
 Germplasm: The total genetic complement of an organism or population, conserved in gene banks for future breeding use. 
Digestibility: The proportion of food that is absorbed by the animal after it has been eaten and passed through the digestive tract. 
 Net Energy: The amount of energy in feed that is actually available for maintenance and production after losses in feces, urine, gas, and heat. 
 Estrus: The period in the reproductive cycle of female mammals (except primates) during which they are sexually receptive and "in heat." 
 Anestrus: A period of sexual inactivity in the female between two breeding seasons or during pregnancy and lactation. 
 Artificial Insemination (AI): The deliberate introduction of sperm into a female's cervix or uterine cavity for the purpose of achieving a pregnancy through in vivo fertilization. 
 Metabolizable Energy (ME): The gross energy of feed minus the energy lost in feces, urine, and combustible gases. 
 Rumen Fermentation: The anaerobic process by which bacteria, protozoa, and fungi break down complex carbohydrates like cellulose into volatile fatty acids (VFAs). 
 Volatile Fatty Acids (VFAs): The primary energy source for ruminants, consisting mainly of acetic, propionic, and butyric acids produced in the rumen. 
 Broiler: A chicken raised specifically for meat production, usually reaching market weight within 6 to 8 weeks. 
 Layer: A female chicken raised primarily for egg production. 
Standard Error: A measure of the statistical accuracy of an estimate, equal to the standard deviation of the theoretical distribution of a large population of such estimates. 
 Analysis of Variance (ANOVA): A statistical method used to test the differences between two or more means, essential for analyzing field trial data. 
 Randomized Block Design (RBD): An experimental design where the area is divided into blocks (replications) and treatments are assigned randomly within each block to minimize soil heterogeneity. 
 Market Surplus: The portion of total agricultural production that is available for sale by the farmer after meeting their own consumption and requirements. 
 Fixed Assets: Assets that are purchased for long-term use and are not likely to be converted quickly into cash, such as land, buildings, and heavy machinery. 
 Variable Assets: Assets that change in quantity during the production cycle, such as seeds, fertilizers, and fuel. 
 Marginal Product: The additional output produced by using one more unit of a variable input, while keeping all other inputs constant. 
 Farm Budgeting: A process of estimating the expected costs and returns for a farm business for a specific period. 
 Agribusiness: The group of industries involved in the producing, processing, and distribution of agricultural products and farm supplies. 
Blanching: A process of briefly plunging vegetables into boiling water to inactivate enzymes that cause spoilage before freezing or drying. 

Curing: A post-harvest treatment used to toughen the skin and heal wounds on root crops like potatoes and onions to increase storage life. 

Water Activity ($a_w$): The ratio of the water vapor pressure of a food to the vapor pressure of pure water; it determines the shelf life and microbial stability of food. 

Aseptic Packaging: The process by which a sterile product is packaged in a sterile container in a way that maintains sterility. 

Fortification: The practice of deliberately increasing the content of an essential micronutrient (vitamins and minerals) in a food to improve its nutritional quality. 

Cold Storage: The storage of perishable products at low temperatures, but above freezing, to slow down metabolic activities and decay. 

Pectin: A structural heteropolysaccharide found in the primary cell walls of terrestrial plants, used commercially as a gelling agent in jams.

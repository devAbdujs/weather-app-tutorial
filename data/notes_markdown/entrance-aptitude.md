Verbal Reasoning:Tests ability to comprehend and analyze written material, including understanding and evaluating arguments, and drawing conclusions
Quantitative Reasoning:Assesses basic mathematical skills and understanding of elementary concepts in algebra, arithmetic, geometry, and data interpretation
Logical/Analytical Reasoning:Evaluates the ability to analyze information and draw logical conclusions based on given premises or rules
Critical Thinking:Involves evaluating arguments, identifying assumptions, and recognizing implications in a logical and systematic manner.
Data Interpretation:Requires understanding and interpreting data presented in various formats such as tables, graphs, and charts.
English Language Proficiency:Tests grammar, vocabulary, comprehension, and writing skills necessary for academic and professional communication.
Problem Solving:Challenges test-takers with scenarios that require applying quantitative and analytical skills to solve real-world problems
Time Management:Effective allocation of time is crucial as the test is typically timed, requiring efficient management of sections and questions
Abstract Reasoning: Assesses logical reasoning ability using non-verbal and diagrammatic questions to analyze patterns and relationships
Cognitive Skills: Assesses higher-order thinking skills such as critical thinking, problem-solving, decision-making, and creative thinking
Graphical Analysis:Involves interpreting data from charts, graphs, and diagrams to extract meaningful insights and draw conclusions
Reading Comprehension:Evaluates understanding of written passages, requiring inference, identifying main ideas, and interpreting information
Problem-Solving Strategies: Includes understanding different strategies to approach complex problems and apply appropriate methods for efficient solutions
Null Hypothesis: The hypothesis that there is no significant difference between specified populations, typically tested against an alternative hypothesis
Standard Deviation: Measure of the dispersion or variability of a set of data points around the mean
Regression Analysis: Statistical method used to determine relationships between variables and predict outcomes
Correlation Coefficient: Measure of the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables
Sample Size: Number of observations or individuals measured or studied in a particular research study or experiment
ANOVA (Analysis of Variance): Statistical method used to analyze the differences between group means in experimental research
Mean: The average of a set of numbers, calculated by dividing the sum of all values by the number of values
Median: The middle value in a sorted, ascending or descending, list of numbers. If there is an even number of observations, the median is the average of the two middle numbers
Mode: The value that appears most frequently in a data set
Range: The difference between the highest and lowest values in a data set
Variance: A measure of how far each number in the set is from the mean. It is calculated by taking the average of the squared differences from the mean
Correlation: A statistical measure that indicates the extent to which two or more variables fluctuate together
Probability: A measure quantifying the likelihood of a specific event occurring, ranging from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain)
Conditional Probability: The probability of an event occurring given that another event has already occurred, denoted as P(A | B)
Independent Events: Events where the occurrence of one event does not affect the probability of the other event occurring
Complementary Events: Events that encompass all possible outcomes of an experiment, such that one event's probability is 1 minus the probability of the other event
Percentage Change: The relative change in quantity over time, expressed as a percentage of its initial value
Mode of a Distribution: The value that appears most frequently in a data set
Standard Error: An estimate of the standard deviation of a sample statistic, indicating the precision of the estimate
Cumulative Frequency: The sum of the frequencies of all data values less than or equal to a specified value in a frequency distribution.
Analogies: A type of verbal reasoning question that requires identifying the relationship between a pair of words and selecting another pair with a similar relationship
 Antonyms and Synonyms: Vocabulary-based questions that test the ability to identify words with opposite or similar meanings, crucial for the verbal proficiency section
 Sentence Completion: Questions that require choosing the most appropriate word or phrase to fill in blanks, testing both vocabulary and contextual understanding
 Arithmetic Word Problems: Mathematical scenarios requiring the application of basic operations, percentages, ratios, and averages to find solutions for real-world situations
 Algebraic Expressions: The use of variables and symbols to represent numbers in equations and inequalities, a core component of quantitative reasoning
 Geometric Theorems: Fundamental rules involving properties of lines, angles, triangles, circles, and polygons used to solve spatial reasoning problems
Data Sufficiency: A unique quantitative question type where the goal is to determine if the provided statements contain enough information to answer a specific question
 Syllogisms: Logical arguments where a conclusion is drawn from two or more premises; for example, 'All mammals are warm-blooded. A dog is a mammal. Therefore, a dog is warm-blooded'
Inductive Reasoning: The process of making generalized conclusions based on specific observations or patterns, often used in sequence-based questions
 Deductive Reasoning: The logical process of reaching a specific conclusion from general premises or established facts
 Analytical Writing: An essay-based section that evaluates the ability to articulate complex ideas, maintain a coherent argument, and use standard written English effectively
 Argument Analysis: The skill of identifying the underlying assumptions, strengths, and weaknesses of a given statement or logical claim
 Probability Distributions: Concepts like the Normal Distribution (Bell Curve) that describe how data values are spread, often tested in advanced data interpretation
Permutations and Combinations: Mathematical techniques used to calculate the number of ways items can be arranged or selected from a group
 Linear Equations and Graphs: The study of relationships between two variables that produce a straight line when plotted, including slope and intercept concepts
Inference: The act of drawing a logical conclusion from information that is suggested or implied rather than explicitly stated in a text
 Critical Reading Strategies: Techniques such as skimming for main ideas and scanning for specific details to improve speed and accuracy in reading comprehension
 Set Theory: The branch of mathematics that studies collections of objects, using concepts like union, intersection, and subsets to solve logical grouping problems
 Logical Fallacies: Common errors in reasoning, such as circular arguments or 'ad hominem' attacks, which test-takers must identify in critical thinking sections
 Back-solving Strategy: A quantitative technique where you plug the provided answer choices back into the original question to find the one that works

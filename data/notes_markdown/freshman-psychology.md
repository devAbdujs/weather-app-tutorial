Psychology: The scientific study of mind, behavior, and mental processes in humans and other animals, using empirical methods to understand, predict, and modify behavior
Wilhelm Wundt: Founder of experimental psychology who established the first psychology laboratory in Leipzig, Germany in 1879, using introspection as primary method
Structuralism: Early school of psychology founded by Wundt and Titchener, focused on analyzing the basic elements or structures of conscious experience through introspection
Functionalism: School of psychology pioneered by William James emphasizing the adaptive functions of mental processes and behavior, influenced by Darwin's evolutionary theory
Behaviorism: School founded by John B. Watson focusing exclusively on observable behavior and environmental determinants, rejecting the study of internal mental states
Classical Conditioning: A form of associative learning discovered by Pavlov where a neutral stimulus becomes associated with a meaningful stimulus to elicit a conditioned response (CS→CR)
Unconditioned Stimulus (UCS): A stimulus that naturally and automatically triggers a response without any prior learning (e.g., food causing salivation)
Unconditioned Response (UCR): The natural, unlearned reaction to the unconditioned stimulus (e.g., salivation to food)
Conditioned Stimulus (CS): A previously neutral stimulus that, after repeated pairing with the UCS, comes to elicit the conditioned response
Conditioned Response (CR): The learned response to the conditioned stimulus that resembles the unconditioned response
Extinction: The gradual weakening and disappearance of a conditioned response when the conditioned stimulus is repeatedly presented without the unconditioned stimulus
Spontaneous Recovery: The reappearance of an extinguished conditioned response after a rest period, without further conditioning
Stimulus Generalization: The tendency for stimuli similar to the conditioned stimulus to elicit the conditioned response
Stimulus Discrimination: The ability to distinguish between the conditioned stimulus and similar stimuli, responding only to the specific CS
Higher-Order Conditioning: A procedure in which a conditioned stimulus is used to condition a new neutral stimulus, creating a second-order CS
Operant Conditioning: B.F. Skinner's learning framework where voluntary behaviors are strengthened or weakened by their consequences (reinforcement and punishment)
Positive Reinforcement: Adding a pleasant stimulus following a behavior to increase the likelihood of that behavior recurring
Negative Reinforcement: Removing an aversive stimulus following a behavior to increase the likelihood of that behavior (NOT punishment)
Positive Punishment: Adding an aversive stimulus following a behavior to decrease its likelihood
Negative Punishment: Removing a pleasant stimulus following a behavior to decrease its likelihood
Fixed Ratio Schedule: A reinforcement schedule providing reinforcement after a fixed number of responses; produces high response rates with post-reinforcement pauses
Variable Ratio Schedule: Reinforcement provided after an unpredictable average number of responses; produces the highest and most consistent response rates, greatest resistance to extinction
Fixed Interval Schedule: Reinforcement provided for the first response after a fixed time period; produces scalloped response pattern
Variable Interval Schedule: Reinforcement provided for the first response after a variable time period; produces steady, moderate response rates
Shaping: The operant conditioning procedure of reinforcing successive approximations toward a desired behavior
Chaining: Linking a sequence of behaviors together, with each behavior serving as both a reinforced response and a discriminative stimulus for the next
Extinction Burst: The initial increase in frequency and intensity of a behavior when reinforcement is first removed before eventual extinction
Social Learning Theory: Albert Bandura's theory proposing that people learn through observing others, emphasizing observational learning, imitation, and modeling
Observational Learning: Learning that occurs by watching and imitating the behavior of models, involving attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation
Self-Efficacy: Bandura's concept of a person's belief in their ability to successfully perform a specific task or achieve a goal in a particular domain
Reciprocal Determinism: Bandura's model proposing bidirectional interaction between behavior, personal factors (cognitions), and environmental influences
Bobo Doll Experiment: Bandura's (1961) classic study demonstrating that children who observed an adult behaving aggressively toward a doll subsequently imitated the aggressive behavior
Psychoanalytic Theory: Freud's comprehensive theory of personality emphasizing unconscious processes, childhood experiences, and internal conflicts as drivers of behavior
Id: Freud's entirely unconscious reservoir of instinctual drives operating on the pleasure principle, seeking immediate gratification
Ego: Freud's reality-oriented component of the psyche operating on the reality principle, mediating between id impulses and external demands
Superego: Freud's internalized moral conscience representing idealized standards and prohibitions developed through socialization
Conscious: The portion of mental life containing thoughts and perceptions that are currently in awareness
Preconscious: Mental content that is not currently in awareness but can be readily brought into consciousness
Unconscious: The largest portion of mental life containing repressed desires, memories, and conflicts outside conscious awareness
Defense Mechanisms: Unconscious psychological strategies used by the ego to protect against anxiety by distorting or denying reality
Repression: The primary defense mechanism involving the involuntary exclusion of threatening thoughts, memories, or impulses from conscious awareness
Projection: Attributing one's own unacceptable thoughts or feelings to others
Rationalization: Creating logical explanations for behaviors that are actually driven by unacceptable impulses
Denial: Refusing to acknowledge painful or anxiety-provoking aspects of reality
Displacement: Redirecting emotional responses from the original threatening target to a safer substitute target
Sublimation: Channeling unacceptable impulses into socially acceptable activities
Reaction Formation: Expressing the opposite of one's true unconscious feelings
Regression: Reverting to earlier, more immature patterns of behavior under stress
Projective Identification: A primitive defense mechanism where a person not only attributes unwanted aspects to another but also behaves to pressure the other to enact those projected qualities
Oral Stage: Freud's first psychosexual stage (0-18 months) where pleasure is centered on feeding and oral stimulation
Anal Stage: Freud's second psychosexual stage (18 months-3 years) where pleasure is focused on bowel control
Phallic Stage: Freud's third psychosexual stage (3-6 years) centered on genitals; Oedipus/Electra complex resolved here
Oedipus Complex: Freud's concept describing a boy's unconscious sexual attraction to his mother and rivalry with his father during the phallic stage
Electra Complex: Jung's concept (later adopted) describing a girl's unconscious attraction to her father and rivalry with her mother
Castration Anxiety: In Freud's theory, the boy's fear of genital mutilation by the father as punishment for Oedipal desires
Penis Envy: Freud's controversial concept suggesting that girls develop envy upon realizing they lack a penis
Latency Stage: Freud's fourth psychosexual stage (6 years to puberty) where sexual impulses are dormant and social skills develop
Genital Stage: Freud's fifth psychosexual stage (puberty onward) characterized by mature sexual interests and relationships
Fixation: In Freudian theory, the persistent focus of psychic energy on an earlier developmental stage due to unresolved conflicts
Transference: The unconscious redirection of feelings and relational patterns from earlier significant relationships onto the therapist
Countertransference: The therapist's unconscious emotional reactions to the client, which may reflect the therapist's own unresolved material or realistic reactions to client dynamics
Transference Neurosis: In psychoanalysis, the development of an artificial neurosis during treatment where the patient's central conflicts become focused on the therapeutic relationship
Free Association: The psychoanalytic technique where patients report any thoughts that come to mind without censorship
Dream Analysis: Freud's technique for accessing the unconscious by interpreting the manifest content (remembered images) and latent content (hidden symbolic meaning) of dreams
Parapraxis (Freudian Slip): An error in speech, memory, or action believed to reveal unconscious thoughts
Erikson's Psychosocial Stages: Eight stages of development across the lifespan, each involving a central psychosocial crisis whose resolution determines psychological health
Trust vs. Mistrust: Erikson's Stage 1 (infancy) — developing basic trust in caregivers leads to hope; failure leads to fear and mistrust
Autonomy vs. Shame and Doubt: Erikson's Stage 2 (toddlerhood) — developing independence leads to will; failure leads to shame
Initiative vs. Guilt: Erikson's Stage 3 (preschool) — developing purpose through exploration; failure leads to excessive guilt
Industry vs. Inferiority: Erikson's Stage 4 (school age) — developing competence; failure leads to feelings of inadequacy
Identity vs. Role Confusion: Erikson's Stage 5 (adolescence) — developing a coherent sense of self; failure leads to identity confusion
Intimacy vs. Isolation: Erikson's Stage 6 (young adulthood) — forming intimate relationships; failure leads to loneliness
Generativity vs. Stagnation: Erikson's Stage 7 (middle adulthood) — contributing to society and the next generation; failure leads to stagnation
Ego Integrity vs. Despair: Erikson's Stage 8 (late adulthood) — accepting one's life as meaningful; failure leads to despair about death and unfulfilled life
Identity Capital: James Côté's concept of personal and social resources accumulated through identity formation — both tangible (credentials, networks) and intangible (self-understanding, cognitive complexity) — facilitating navigation of adult transitions
Marcia's Identity Statuses: Four identity outcomes based on exploration and commitment: Identity Diffusion, Identity Foreclosure, Identity Moratorium, and Identity Achievement
Piaget's Cognitive Development Theory: A stage theory proposing that children develop through four qualitatively different stages of thinking as they interact with their environment
Sensorimotor Stage: Piaget's Stage 1 (0-2 years) — knowledge through senses and actions; object permanence develops
Preoperational Stage: Piaget's Stage 2 (2-7 years) — symbolic thinking emerges; thought is egocentric, lacks conservation and logical operations
Concrete Operational Stage: Piaget's Stage 3 (7-11 years) — logical reasoning about concrete objects; conservation, classification, seriation develop
Formal Operational Stage: Piaget's Stage 4 (12+ years) — abstract, hypothetical, and systematic thinking emerges
Object Permanence: The understanding that objects continue to exist even when they cannot be directly perceived; develops around 8-12 months
Conservation: The understanding that quantity remains constant despite changes in appearance; develops during concrete operations
Egocentrism: In Piaget's theory, the preoperational child's inability to take the perspective of others
Assimilation: Incorporating new information into existing schemas
Accommodation: Modifying existing schemas or creating new ones to fit new information
Schema: Mental frameworks of organized knowledge that guide perception, interpretation, and memory
Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): Vygotsky's concept of the range of tasks a learner can accomplish with guidance but not independently — the optimal zone for learning
Scaffolding: The support provided by a more capable person that is gradually withdrawn as the learner develops competence
Private Speech: Vygotsky's concept that children's self-directed speech while solving problems gradually becomes internalized as verbal thought
Kohlberg's Moral Development: Three levels of moral reasoning — Preconventional (self-interest, avoiding punishment), Conventional (social norms, maintaining order), Postconventional (universal ethical principles)
Heinz Dilemma: Kohlberg's classic moral dilemma used to assess moral reasoning stage by analyzing the justification for whether Heinz should steal a drug to save his wife
Gilligan's Ethics of Care: Carol Gilligan's critique of Kohlberg arguing that women's moral reasoning emphasizes relationships and care rather than abstract justice, representing a valid alternative moral framework
Attachment Theory: Bowlby's theory that infants have a biological predisposition to form close emotional bonds with caregivers, providing a secure base for exploration
Secure Attachment: The healthiest attachment pattern where infants use the caregiver as a secure base and are easily soothed upon reunion
Anxious/Ambivalent Attachment: Attachment pattern characterized by excessive protest at separation and difficulty being soothed upon reunion, reflecting uncertainty about caregiver availability
Avoidant Attachment: Attachment pattern characterized by minimal distress at separation and avoidance of the caregiver upon reunion, reflecting learned suppression of attachment needs
Disorganized Attachment: Attachment pattern with no coherent strategy for managing distress, associated with abuse or neglect; caregiver is simultaneously source of fear and comfort
Internal Working Models: Bowlby's concept of cognitive-affective schemas of self, others, and relationships formed from early attachment experiences that guide later relationships
Strange Situation: Mary Ainsworth's laboratory procedure using brief separations and reunions to assess infant attachment style
Adult Attachment Styles: Hazan and Shaver's extension of Ainsworth's attachment styles to adult romantic relationships: Secure, Anxious/Preoccupied, Avoidant/Dismissing, and Fearful-Avoidant
Mentalizing/Reflective Functioning: Fonagy's concept of the capacity to understand behavior in terms of underlying mental states; its impairment underlies borderline personality disorder and other trauma-related conditions
Mentalization-Based Treatment (MBT): Fonagy and Bateman's evidence-based therapy for BPD targeting restoration of mentalizing capacity
Sensation: The biological process by which sensory receptors detect physical stimuli and transmit neural signals to the brain
Perception: The psychological process of organizing, interpreting, and making meaning from sensory information
Absolute Threshold: The minimum stimulus intensity detectable 50% of the time under ideal conditions
Difference Threshold (JND): The minimum change in stimulus intensity that can be detected; Weber's Law states JND is a constant proportion of the original stimulus
Signal Detection Theory: A framework for understanding how people detect stimuli under conditions of uncertainty, separating sensitivity from response bias (hits, misses, false alarms, correct rejections)
Gestalt Principles: Principles of perceptual organization — Proximity, Similarity, Closure, Continuity, Figure-Ground, Common Fate
Depth Perception: The ability to perceive the three-dimensional structure and distance of objects using binocular cues (retinal disparity, convergence) and monocular cues (linear perspective, relative size, texture gradient, interposition)
Retinal Disparity: The slight difference in images from each eye that provides the primary binocular cue for depth perception
Müller-Lyer Illusion: Two lines of equal length appearing different due to arrow configurations; less susceptible in non-carpentered cultures, demonstrating cultural influences on perception
Atkinson-Shiffrin Multi-Store Model: Three-stage model of memory: Sensory memory → Short-term memory → Long-term memory, with transfer through rehearsal
Working Memory Model (Baddeley and Hitch): Multi-component model of active memory processing: Central Executive, Phonological Loop, Visuospatial Sketchpad, Episodic Buffer
Central Executive: The attentional control system of working memory that coordinates the other components and allocates resources
Phonological Loop: Working memory component that stores and rehearses speech-based information
Visuospatial Sketchpad: Working memory component that stores and manipulates visual and spatial information
Episodic Buffer: Working memory component (added 2000) that integrates information from multiple sources into a coherent episode
Long-Term Memory: Potentially unlimited memory store; divided into explicit (declarative) and implicit (non-declarative) memory
Declarative Memory: Explicit memory requiring conscious recollection; includes episodic (personal events) and semantic (factual knowledge) memory
Episodic Memory: Memory for personally experienced events with temporal and contextual details — mental time travel; hippocampus-dependent
Semantic Memory: Memory for general factual knowledge independent of personal experience; more distributed cortically
Procedural Memory: Implicit memory for skills and habits performed automatically without conscious recollection; basal ganglia-dependent
Priming: The implicit memory phenomenon where prior exposure to a stimulus facilitates processing of the same or related stimulus without conscious awareness
Implicit Learning: The unconscious, incidental acquisition of knowledge about regularities in the environment without intentional effort or awareness (Reber's artificial grammar learning)
Serial Position Effect: Better memory for items at the beginning (primacy — LTM transfer) and end (recency — STM) of a list than for middle items
Encoding Specificity Principle: Memory retrieval is best when conditions at recall match conditions at encoding (state-dependent and context-dependent memory)
Flashbulb Memory: Vivid, detailed memories of circumstances surrounding emotionally significant and surprising events; feel permanent but are not more accurate than ordinary memories
Misinformation Effect: The distortion of original memory by post-event misleading information (Loftus's car crash experiments)
False Memory: A vivid memory for an event that never occurred; can be implanted through suggestion, imagination inflation, and leading questions (Loftus's 'Lost in a Mall' study)
Levels of Processing (Craik and Lockhart): Deeper, more meaningful processing (semantic) produces better memory than shallow (structural) processing
Elaborative Interrogation: A learning strategy involving asking and answering 'why' questions about material to promote deep encoding and long-term retention
HM (Henry Molaison): Famous amnesic patient whose bilateral hippocampectomy demonstrated the hippocampus's critical role in forming new declarative memories; retained procedural memory and priming
Proactive Interference: Old memories interfere with learning or recalling new information
Retroactive Interference: New learning interferes with recall of old information
Anterograde Amnesia: Inability to form new long-term memories after brain damage
Retrograde Amnesia: Inability to recall memories formed before brain damage; typically shows temporal gradient with more recent memories most affected
Neuroplasticity: The brain's ability to change structurally and functionally in response to experience, learning, and injury
Dopamine: Neurotransmitter associated with reward, motivation, pleasure, movement; implicated in addiction (mesolimbic pathway), schizophrenia (dopamine hypothesis), and Parkinson's disease (nigrostriatal pathway)
Serotonin: Neurotransmitter regulating mood, sleep, appetite, and impulse control; implicated in depression and anxiety; targeted by SSRIs
Norepinephrine: Neurotransmitter involved in arousal, attention, and stress response; targeted by SNRIs and some antidepressants
GABA: The primary inhibitory neurotransmitter of the CNS; targeted by benzodiazepines (anxiolytics) and alcohol
Glutamate: The primary excitatory neurotransmitter; implicated in learning, memory (LTP), and the mechanisms of ketamine's rapid antidepressant effects
Acetylcholine: Neurotransmitter involved in muscle movement, learning, and memory; degraded in Alzheimer's disease
BDNF (Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor): A protein supporting neuron survival and growth; reduced in depression and increased by antidepressants and exercise
Amygdala: Almond-shaped limbic structure critical for processing fear, threat detection, emotional memory, and emotional responses
Hippocampus: Seahorse-shaped limbic structure critical for consolidating new declarative memories, spatial navigation, and contextual learning; damaged early in Alzheimer's disease and in depression
Prefrontal Cortex (PFC): Frontal lobe region critical for executive functions (planning, decision-making, inhibition, working memory), emotional regulation, and social cognition; fully developed by mid-20s
Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC): Brain region involved in error detection, conflict monitoring, emotional regulation, and attention; implicated in depression and OCD
Hypothalamus: Brain structure regulating hunger, thirst, body temperature, sex drive, and circadian rhythms; controls the pituitary gland (HPA axis)
Limbic System: A group of interconnected brain structures (amygdala, hippocampus, hypothalamus, thalamus) involved in emotion, motivation, and memory
Cerebral Cortex: The outer layer of the cerebrum responsible for higher cognitive functions; divided into four lobes
Frontal Lobe: Contains motor cortex and Broca's area (speech production); responsible for voluntary movement, executive functions, and personality
Parietal Lobe: Processes somatosensory information (touch, temperature, pain); involved in spatial awareness; contains somatosensory cortex
Temporal Lobe: Processes auditory information; contains Wernicke's area (language comprehension) and hippocampus; involved in memory
Occipital Lobe: Dedicated to visual processing
Broca's Area: Left frontal lobe region involved in speech production; damage causes Broca's aphasia (labored, non-fluent speech with intact comprehension)
Wernicke's Area: Left temporal lobe region involved in language comprehension; damage causes Wernicke's aphasia (fluent but meaningless speech)
Phineas Gage: Historical case of frontal lobe damage demonstrating the role of the prefrontal cortex in personality, decision-making, and social behavior
Sympathetetic Nervous System: Division of the ANS activating the fight-or-flight response; increases heart rate, blood pressure, diverts blood to muscles
Parasympathetic Nervous System: Division of the ANS promoting rest-and-digest; slows heart rate, stimulates digestion
HPA Axis: Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal axis — the neuroendocrine stress response system releasing cortisol; chronic activation damages hippocampus and suppresses immunity
Cortisol: The primary glucocorticoid stress hormone released by the adrenal cortex via HPA axis activation; mobilizes energy, suppresses immunity
General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS): Selye's three-stage model of physiological stress response — Alarm, Resistance, Exhaustion
Twin Studies: Research comparing MZ (100% shared DNA) and DZ (50% shared DNA) twins to estimate heritability of psychological traits
Heritability: A statistical estimate of the proportion of variation in a trait within a population attributable to genetic differences; context-specific, does not mean fixed
Flynn Effect: The documented rise in population IQ scores over the 20th century, demonstrating substantial environmental influence on intelligence
Fluid Intelligence (Gf): The ability to reason with novel information, peaks in early adulthood and gradually declines
Crystallized Intelligence (Gc): Accumulated knowledge and verbal ability, remains stable or grows into late adulthood
General Intelligence (g): Spearman's concept of a general factor underlying performance on diverse cognitive tasks
Multiple Intelligences: Gardner's theory proposing eight distinct intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic
Triarchic Theory of Intelligence: Sternberg's model proposing three types: analytical, creative, and practical intelligence
Emotional Intelligence (EI): Goleman's concept of the ability to perceive, understand, manage, and use emotions effectively; five components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills
IQ (Intelligence Quotient): A standardized score representing cognitive ability relative to age peers; average set at 100, SD of 15; uses deviation IQ not ratio IQ
Deviation IQ: The modern IQ calculation comparing an individual's performance to their same-age peers rather than mental age/chronological age ratio
Mental Age: Binet's concept of the age-level at which a child's performance matches the average; original basis for ratio IQ (MA/CA × 100)
Stereotype Threat: The situational predicament where awareness of a negative group stereotype impairs performance through cognitive interference and anxiety (Steele and Aronson)
Sensation-Seeking: The tendency to seek novel, intense, and varied experiences; associated with risk-taking behavior
Big Five Personality Traits (OCEAN): The five broad dimensions of personality — Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism — identified through factor analysis
Neuroticism: The Big Five trait of emotional instability, anxiety proneness, and moodiness; the strongest personality predictor of mental health problems
Conscientious: The Big Five trait of organization, reliability, and self-discipline; the broadest predictor of academic and occupational success and longevity
Extraversion: The Big Five trait of sociability and positive affect; associated with BAS sensitivity and subjective well-being
Agreeableness: The Big Five trait of cooperativeness, empathy, and trust; predicts quality of social relationships
Openness to Experience: The Big Five trait of curiosity, creativity, and openness to new ideas; predicts creative achievement
Jungian Psychological Types: Carl Jung's personality dimensions: Extraversion-Introversion attitude types and four functions (Thinking-Feeling; Sensation-Intuition); basis for MBTI
Personality Disorders: Enduring, pervasive patterns of inner experience and behavior deviating from cultural norms, causing significant impairment across multiple domains
Cluster A Personality Disorders: Odd/eccentric cluster: Paranoid, Schizoid, Schizotypal
Cluster B Personality Disorders: Dramatic/erratic cluster: Antisocial, Borderline, Histrionic, Narcissistic
Cluster C Personality Disorders: Anxious/fearful cluster: Avoidant, Dependent, Obsessive-Compulsive
Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD): Pervasive instability in relationships, self-image, and emotions; intense fear of abandonment; impulsivity; self-harm; identity disturbance; treated with DBT
Dark Triad: Three antisocial personality traits identified by Paulhus and Williams: Narcissism (grandiosity), Machiavellianism (strategic manipulation), Psychopathy (callousness, impulsivity)
Locus of Control: Rotter's personality dimension — internal (believing one controls outcomes) vs. external (outcomes determined by luck or powerful others)
Learned Helplessness: Seligman's concept of passive behavior resulting from learning that responses are ineffective; basis for his attributional model of depression
Self-Efficacy: Bandura's domain-specific belief in one's capacity to perform a behavior successfully; predicts effort, persistence, and achievement
Self-Determination Theory (SDT): Deci and Ryan's theory proposing three innate psychological needs — Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness — whose satisfaction promotes optimal functioning
Need Frustration vs. Need Satisfaction: Vansteenkiste and Ryan's distinction — need satisfaction actively promotes well-being while need frustration (active thwarting of needs) produces unique ill-being outcomes
Intrinsic Motivation: Engaging in an activity for its inherent interest and enjoyment; supported by autonomy and competence
Extrinsic Motivation: Engaging in an activity for external rewards or pressures; can undermine intrinsic motivation (overjustification effect)
Overjustification Effect: The finding that introducing expected external rewards for previously intrinsically motivated activities decreases subsequent intrinsic motivation
Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs: A five-level motivational pyramid: Physiological → Safety → Love/Belonging → Esteem → Self-Actualization
Self-Actualization: The highest level in Maslow's hierarchy representing the realization of one's full potential and personal growth
Hunger and Eating: Regulation involves the hypothalamus (lateral — hunger; ventromedial — satiety), leptin, ghrelin, insulin, and psychological factors
Sexual Motivation: Influenced by hormones (testosterone, estrogen), neural systems, cultural factors, and individual differences in sexual orientation
Emotion: A complex state involving physiological arousal, subjective experience, cognitive appraisal, and behavioral expression
James-Lange Theory: Physiological arousal occurs first, then the person perceives this arousal and labels it as an emotion ('we feel afraid because we tremble')
Cannon-Bard Theory: Physiological arousal and emotional experience occur simultaneously and independently via thalamic activation of cortex and body
Schachter-Singer Two-Factor Theory: Emotion results from general physiological arousal + cognitive labeling based on situational context
Appraisal Theory (Lazarus): Emotions result from cognitive evaluation of a situation relative to personal goals and coping capacity
LeDoux's Two-Route Model: Fast subcortical pathway (thalamus → amygdala) for rapid fear responses before cortical (conscious) processing; slow cortical route for detailed analysis
Polyvagal Theory (Porges): Three-level ANS hierarchy: Ventral vagal (social engagement/safety), Sympathetic (fight/flight), Dorsal vagal (freeze/collapse); neuroception continuously evaluates safety
Basic Emotions: Six universal facial expressions recognized cross-culturally: happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, disgust (Ekman)
Display Rules: Culturally determined norms governing how and when emotions are appropriately expressed
Negative Affect: A general dimension of experiencing negative emotional states; strongly linked to neuroticism and mental health vulnerability
Positive Affect: A general dimension of experiencing positive emotional states; associated with extraversion and well-being
Stress: The physiological and psychological response when perceived demands exceed available coping resources
Eustress: Positive, motivating stress that enhances performance and functioning
Distress: Negative stress that exceeds coping capacity and produces harmful outcomes
Primary Appraisal: Lazarus's first stage of stress evaluation — is this situation a threat, challenge, or irrelevant?
Secondary Appraisal: Lazarus's second stage — do I have sufficient resources to cope?
Coping Strategies: Problem-focused coping (addressing the stressor) vs. emotion-focused coping (managing emotional response to the stressor)
Social Support: Perceived availability of assistance from others; the most consistently identified resilience factor
Resilience: The dynamic process of positive adaptation within significant adversity; supported by social support, self-efficacy, emotion regulation, and meaning-making
Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG): Positive psychological change experienced through struggling with highly challenging circumstances — beyond resilience to transformation (Tedeschi and Calhoun)
ACE Study: Adverse Childhood Experiences Study (Felitti and Anda) demonstrating a dose-response relationship between childhood trauma and adult physical/mental health outcomes
Major Depressive Disorder (MDD): Characterized by depressed mood or anhedonia plus at least five symptoms for two weeks; includes changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, guilt, and suicidal ideation
Bipolar Disorder: Characterized by episodes of mania (or hypomania) AND depression; antidepressants alone can trigger mania
Mania: Elevated or irritable mood, decreased sleep need, grandiosity, racing thoughts, increased goal-directed activity, impulsivity; lasting at least one week (Bipolar I)
Anhedonia: Loss of interest or pleasure in previously enjoyed activities; a core symptom of depression and negative symptom of schizophrenia
Cognitive Triad (Beck): Beck's three characteristic negative thought patterns in depression: negative views of the self, the world, and the future
Cognitive Distortions: Systematic errors in thinking maintaining psychological disorders — overgeneralization, selective abstraction, arbitrary inference, magnification/minimization, personalization, all-or-nothing thinking
Depressive Realism: The finding by Alloy and Abramson that mildly depressed individuals show more accurate (less inflated) perceptions of control than non-depressed individuals — 'sadder but wiser'
Attributional Style: The tendency to explain events using internal/external, stable/unstable, global/specific causal attributions; pessimistic style predicts depression
Rumination: Repetitive, passive focus on distress symptoms and their causes without moving toward active coping; Nolen-Hoeksema's key maintaining factor in depression
Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD): Persistent, excessive, uncontrollable worry about multiple topics causing significant impairment; associated with overestimation of threat and intolerance of uncertainty
Panic Disorder: Recurrent unexpected panic attacks plus persistent concern about future attacks; Clark's cognitive model: catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations
Agoraphobia: Fear of situations where escape might be difficult if panic occurs; often develops as a consequence of panic disorder
Social Anxiety Disorder: Intense fear of social situations involving potential scrutiny; significantly beyond normal shyness; maintained by safety behaviors and self-focused attention
Specific Phobia: Excessive fear of a specific object or situation; maintained by avoidance; treated with graduated exposure
Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD): Ego-dystonic obsessions (unwanted intrusive thoughts) plus compulsions that temporarily relieve anxiety; gold-standard treatment is ERP
OCPD vs. OCD: OCD involves ego-dystonic obsessions causing distress; OCPD involves ego-syntonic perfectionism and control seen as natural to self — fundamentally different conditions
Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD): Preoccupation with imagined or slight defects in physical appearance causing significant distress or impairment
Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD): Develops after trauma exposure; four clusters: intrusion, avoidance, negative cognition/mood, hyperarousal; DSM-5 moved to Trauma/Stressor-Related chapter
Ehlers and Clark's Cognitive Model of PTSD: PTSD is maintained by traumatic appraisals, poorly elaborated trauma memory that triggers intrusions, and problematic coping behaviors that prevent cognitive processing
Acute Stress Response vs. PTSD: Acute stress response is a normal, expected response to trauma; PTSD is diagnosed when symptoms persist beyond one month and cause significant impairment
Complex PTSD (ICD-11): Chronic, repeated trauma (especially interpersonal) produces additional features beyond standard PTSD: affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, relational disturbances
Schizophrenia: A psychotic disorder requiring positive and negative symptoms plus functional impairment for at least six months
Positive Symptoms (Schizophrenia): 'Added' to normal experience — hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking, disorganized behavior
Negative Symptoms (Schizophrenia): 'Absent' from normal functioning — flat affect, alogia, avolition, anhedonia, social withdrawal; more persistent and treatment-resistant
Auditory Hallucinations: The most common psychotic hallucination — hearing voices that others cannot hear
Delusions: Fixed false beliefs not amenable to reason — persecutory, grandiose, referential, control, somatic
Disorganized Thinking: Loose associations, tangentiality, word salad — incoherent speech reflecting formal thought disorder
Dopamine Hypothesis (Schizophrenia): Excess dopamine activity (particularly in mesolimbic system) underlies positive symptoms; supported by dopamine-blocking antipsychotics
Neurodevelopmental Hypothesis: Murray and Lewis's proposal that schizophrenia results from disrupted early brain development, remaining silent until adolescence unmasks it; supported by prenatal risk factors and premorbid deficits
Anhedonia (Schizophrenia): Reduced capacity to experience pleasure; a negative symptom reflecting dopaminergic reward system dysfunction
Social Functioning in Schizophrenia: Negative symptoms produce profound impairment in social relationships and occupational functioning
Antipsychotic Medications: Typical (first-generation, D2 blockers, e.g., haloperidol) and atypical (second-generation, also block serotonin, e.g., olanzapine, clozapine) — treat positive symptoms
EarlyIntervention in Psychosis (EIP): Phase-specific treatment in early psychosis showing improved outcomes during the 'critical period' of first five years
Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD): Neurodevelopmental disorder with inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity across two or more settings, before age 12; associated with PFC underactivation and dopaminergic/noradrenergic deficit
Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD): Neurodevelopmental disorder with persistent deficits in social communication (Domain A) and restricted/repetitive behaviors and interests (Domain B)
Mindblindness: Baron-Cohen's hypothesis that ASD involves a specific impairment in theory of mind (ToM)
Intellectual Disability: Significantly below-average intellectual functioning (IQ below 70) with concurrent deficits in adaptive behavior, originating in the developmental period
Dyslexia: A specific learning disorder involving difficulties with accurate and fluent word reading and decoding, despite adequate intelligence and instruction
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): Structured, short-term therapy targeting cognitive distortions and maladaptive behaviors maintaining psychological disorders; evidence-based for depression, anxiety, OCD, PTSD, and eating disorders
Behavioral Activation (BA): An evidence-based behavioral treatment for depression based on Lewinsohn's model — systematically increasing engagement with positively reinforcing activities to break the withdrawal-depression cycle
Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP): Gold-standard CBT for OCD — exposing patients to obsessive triggers while preventing compulsive response, allowing anxiety to extinguish
Systematic Desensitization: Wolpe's behavioral therapy for phobias — relaxation training, fear hierarchy construction, and gradual exposure while maintaining relaxation (counter-conditioning)
FloodingImplosive Therapy: Immediate, prolonged, maximum-intensity exposure to feared stimulus without graduated approach; allows anxiety to peak and subside through habituation
Cognitive Restructuring: CBT technique identifying and challenging distorted automatic thoughts and underlying beliefs
Behavioral Experiments: CBT technique testing the validity of beliefs through direct behavioral observation
Socratic Questioning: CBT technique using guided questions to help clients examine the evidence for and against their beliefs
Exposure Therapy: The core component of all effective anxiety treatments — systematic approach to feared stimuli without avoidance, allowing extinction learning
Inhibitory Learning Model (Craske): Modern exposure model emphasizing expectancy violation over habituation — maximizing the gap between feared and actual outcome
Person-Centered Therapy (Rogers): Humanistic therapy emphasizing unconditional positive regard, empathy, and congruence as conditions for therapeutic growth
Unconditional Positive Regard: Rogers's core condition of accepting and valuing the client completely without judgment or conditions
Gestalt Therapy: Humanistic therapy emphasizing present-moment awareness, wholeness, and taking responsibility for one's experience
Psychoanalytic/Psychodynamic Therapy: Long-term therapy exploring unconscious conflicts, defense mechanisms, early experiences, and transference to achieve insight
Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT): Linehan's comprehensive therapy for BPD and emotion dysregulation — four skill modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness
DBT Validation: Communicating that the client's feelings and behaviors make sense given their history — not agreeing with harmful behavior but acknowledging the pain driving it
DBT Dialectical Balance: The central DBT dialectic of simultaneous acceptance (validation) and change — holding both poles without collapsing into either
Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT): Third-wave CBT targeting psychological flexibility through six processes: acceptance, defusion, present-moment contact, self-as-context, values, and committed action
Psychological Flexibility: Hayes's core ACT construct — the ability to contact present-moment experience fully and persist in or change behavior in service of chosen values
Experiential Avoidance: The ACT target of unwillingness to experience difficult internal events — struggling against unwanted thoughts, feelings, and sensations, which paradoxically maintains them
Cognitive Defusion: ACT technique of creating distance from thoughts, observing them as mental events rather than literal truths ('I'm having the thought that...')
Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT): Segal, Williams, and Teasdale's integration of mindfulness with CBT for prevention of depressive relapse; reduces relapse by ~43% in those with 3+ prior episodes
Mindfulness: Kabat-Zinn's definition — paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally; changes relationship to thoughts rather than thought content
Schema Therapy: Young's integrative therapy addressing early maladaptive schemas formed from unmet childhood needs; particularly effective for personality disorders and chronic depression
Early Maladaptive Schemas: Young's 18 deeply held negative patterns about self and world formed from childhood — organized into 5 domains including Disconnection/Rejection, Impaired Autonomy
Motivational Interviewing (MI): Miller and Rollnick's collaborative, person-centered counseling style exploring and resolving ambivalence through empathy, developing discrepancy, rolling with resistance, and supporting self-efficacy
Transtheoretical Model (TTM): Prochaska and DiClemente's stage model: Precontemplation, Contemplation, Preparation, Action, Maintenance — change rarely linear
Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT): De Shazer and Berg's future-focused therapy using miracle questions, scaling questions, and exception-finding rather than problem analysis
Narrative Therapy: White and Epston's postmodern therapy using externalization ('the person is not the problem'), dominant narratives, unique outcomes, and re-authoring preferred stories
Interpersonal Therapy (IPT): Klerman and Weissman's structured, time-limited therapy targeting grief, role transitions, interpersonal disputes, and interpersonal deficits; strongly evidence-based for depression
Common Factors: Therapeutic alliance, expectation of improvement, empathy, and shared rationale account for ~40% of outcome variance — common to all effective therapies regardless of modality
Therapeutic Alliance: The quality of the collaborative bond between therapist and client plus agreement on goals and tasks; one of the strongest predictors of therapy outcomes across all modalities
Rupture and Repair: Safran and Muran's finding that skillful resolution of alliance ruptures through metacommunication predicts better outcomes than alliances that never rupture
Dodo Bird Verdict: Luborsky's controversial claim that all bona fide psychotherapy approaches achieve roughly equivalent outcomes — named after Lewis Carroll's dodo who declared 'Everyone has won'
Evidence-Based Practice in Psychology (EBPP): APA's framework integrating best available research evidence + clinical expertise + patient characteristics and preferences
Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT): The gold standard experimental design for establishing treatment efficacy — random assignment to treatment and control conditions
Efficacy vs. Effectiveness: Efficacy: whether treatment works under ideal, controlled conditions; Effectiveness: whether it works in real-world clinical practice with typical patients
Meta-Analysis: A statistical technique combining results from multiple independent studies — produces pooled effect sizes; higher in evidence hierarchy than any single RCT
Effect Size (Cohen's d): A standardized measure of the magnitude of a treatment effect; d = 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium), 0.8 (large)
Replication Crisis: The finding that approximately 36-50% of published psychology findings fail to replicate, driven by publication bias, p-hacking, small samples, and HARKing
Preregistration: Registering hypotheses and analysis plans before data collection to prevent HARKing and p-hacking
Publication Bias: The tendency for positive, statistically significant findings to be published more readily than null results, inflating the literature's effect sizes
P-Hacking: Conducting multiple statistical analyses and reporting only those that produce significant results (p < .05)
HARKing: Hypothesizing After Results are Known — presenting post-hoc explanations as a priori hypotheses
Independent Variable (IV): The variable manipulated by the researcher in an experiment
Dependent Variable (DV): The measured outcome variable expected to change as a result of manipulation of the IV
Confounding Variable: An uncontrolled variable that varies with the IV and may influence the DV, threatening internal validity
Internal Validity: The degree to which changes in the DV can be attributed to manipulation of the IV
External Validity: The degree to which findings generalize beyond the specific study conditions
Demand Characteristics: Cues in the experimental situation that communicate the hypothesis to participants, causing them to change behavior
Hawthorne Effect: The tendency for research participants to change behavior because they know they are being observed
Observer Bias: The unconscious influence of researcher expectations on observation and recording of participant behavior
Blind Procedure: A design where participants are unaware of their condition assignment (single blind) or neither participants nor researcher know (double blind)
Correlation: A statistical relationship between two variables; does not establish causation
Correlation Coefficient (r): Ranges from -1 to +1; magnitude indicates strength, sign indicates direction of the relationship
Causation vs. Correlation: Establishing causation requires: correlation, temporal precedence, and elimination of alternatives — typically requiring experimental design
Naturalistic Observation: Observing behavior in real-world settings without intervention
Case Study: An intensive study of a single individual or small group providing rich detail but limited generalizability
Survey Research: Collecting self-report data from large samples; vulnerable to social desirability bias
Longitudinal Design: Following the same participants over time; reveals developmental change but is time-intensive and subject to attrition
Cross-Sectional Design: Comparing different age groups at one point in time; vulnerable to cohort effects
Debrief: Full disclosure to participants after a study involving deception
Informed Consent: Participants are fully informed and voluntarily agree to participate before the study begins
Confidentiality: Protecting participants' identity and data
Right to Withdraw: Participants can leave at any time without penalty
Fundamental Attribution Error (FAE): The tendency to overestimate dispositional factors and underestimate situational factors when explaining others' behavior
Actor-Observer Effect: Using situational explanations for own behavior but dispositional explanations for others' behavior
Self-Serving Bias: Attributing successes to internal causes (ability, effort) and failures to external causes (luck, difficulty)
Conformity: Changing behavior to match group norms without being explicitly asked (Asch's line judgment experiments)
Informational Social Influence: Conforming because we believe the group has more accurate information
Normative Social Influence: Conforming to gain approval and avoid rejection from the group
Asch Conformity Experiments: Participants conformed to obviously wrong group answers 37% of the time; reduced by social support
Milgram's Obedience Experiments: 65% of participants administered maximum electric shocks when ordered by an authority figure; demonstrated the agentic state and situational power over conscience
Agentic State: Milgram's concept of surrendering personal responsibility to authority, enabling obedience to harmful orders
Obedience: Following direct orders from authority figures
Compliance: Doing what others request; techniques include foot-in-the-door, door-in-the-face
Bystander Effect: Reduced likelihood of helping in emergencies when others are present due to diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance (Darley and Latané)
Diffusion of Responsibility: Each bystander feels less personally obligated to act when others are present
Plurialistic Ignorance: Looking to others for cues in an ambiguous situation; seeing no one else reacting, concluding no emergency exists
Social Loafing: Exerting less effort when working in a group than alone due to reduced individual accountability
Social Facilitation: The presence of others improves performance on easy/well-learned tasks but impairs performance on difficult/novel tasks (Zajonc's drive theory)
Groupthink: Janis's concept of cohesive groups prioritizing consensus over critical thinking; suppressing dissent and producing flawed decisions
Deindividuation: Reduced self-awareness and personal accountability in group settings, weakening inhibitions against antisocial behavior; SIDE model: amplifies salient group norms rather than simply causing antisocial behavior
Cognitive Dissonance: Festinger's theory — mental discomfort from inconsistent beliefs or behaviors motivating change to reduce dissonance
Self-Perception Theory (Bem): We infer our own attitudes from observing our behavior when internal states are ambiguous
Attitudes: Evaluative dispositions toward objects, people, or issues; components: cognitive, affective, behavioral
Persuasion: The process of changing attitudes through communication; ELM (Petty and Cacioppo) identifies central route (deep processing) and peripheral route (heuristic cues)
Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM): Petty and Cacioppo's dual-process model of persuasion — central route produces durable attitude change; peripheral route produces temporary change based on cues
Prejudice: A negative attitude toward a social group member based solely on group membership
Discrimination: Unfair, differential treatment of individuals based on group membership
Stereotype: A cognitive generalization about the characteristics of members of a social group
Implicit Bias: Automatic, unconscious associations influencing judgment outside conscious awareness; measured by IAT
Implicit Association Test (IAT): Greenwald and Banaji's measure of implicit biases through response time differences when pairing concepts
ContactHypothesis: Allport's proposal that intergroup contact reduces prejudice under four conditions: equal status, cooperative goals, institutional support, personal acquaintance
Social Identity Theory: Tajfel and Turner's theory that part of self-concept derives from group membership; in-group favoritism results from the motivation for positive social identity
Minimal Group Paradigm: Tajfel's demonstration that arbitrary group categorization alone produces in-group favoritism — even without interaction, history, or material self-interest
In-Group Favoritism: Preferential treatment of members of one's own group
Out-Group Homogeneity Effect: Perceiving members of out-groups as more similar to each other than members of in-groups
System Justification Theory: The tendency to defend and justify the existing social order, even when it disadvantages one's own group
Ambivalent Sexism: Glick and Fiske's distinction between hostile sexism (antipathy toward non-traditional women) and benevolent sexism (patronizing positive attitudes toward traditional femininity) — both predict gender inequality
Stereotype Threat: The performance impairment in members of stigmatized groups when relevant negative group stereotypes are salient
Self-Concept: The totality of one's beliefs about oneself
Self-Esteem: The evaluative dimension of self-concept — global positive or negative self-regard
Self-Discrepancy Theory: Higgins's model — actual vs. ideal discrepancy produces dejection (depression); actual vs. ought discrepancy produces agitation (anxiety)
Social Comparison Theory: Festinger's theory that people evaluate their opinions and abilities by comparing themselves to others; upward comparisons (to those better off) can inspire or demoralize; downward comparisons enhance self-evaluation
False Consensus Effect: The tendency to overestimate the extent to which others share our beliefs and behaviors
Spotlight Effect: People overestimate how much others notice their appearance and behavior
Illusion of Transparency: People overestimate how visible their internal states are to others
Self-Serving Bias: Attributing successes to self and failures to external factors — protects and enhances self-esteem
Positive Illusions: Slightly inflated self-assessments and optimistic expectations common in healthy individuals; may be protective; contrasted with depressive realism
NarrativeTherapy Self: The idea that identity is a story we tell about ourselves — central to narrative therapy and cultural psychology
Fundamental Attribution Error: Overattributing behavior to disposition rather than situation; weaker in collectivist cultures
Cultural Psychology: Shweder, Markus, and Kitayama's field studying how cultural contexts shape psychological processes
Individualism vs. Collectivism: Hofstede's key cultural dimension; independent self-concept (Western) vs. interdependent self-concept (East Asian); affects attention, attribution, conformity, and self-concept
WEIRD Problem: Henrich's observation that most psychology research is conducted on Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic samples, limiting universality claims
Postcolonial Psychology: Critical examination of how colonialism shaped psychological knowledge and the epistemic injustice embedded in Western-dominated frameworks
Health Psychology: Studies how psychological, behavioral, and social factors influence physical health; biopsychosocial model
Biopsychosocial Model: Engel's framework proposing health and illness result from interactions among biological, psychological, and social factors
Health Belief Model: Rosenstock's model — perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy predict health behavior
Theory of Planned Behavior: Ajzen's model — behavioral intention (predicted by attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control) is the strongest predictor of behavior
Fear Appeals: Effective only when BOTH threat appraisal AND coping appraisal are high; otherwise produces defensive avoidance (Extended Parallel Process Model)
Psychoneuroimmunology (PNI): The interdisciplinary study of interactions among psychological processes, the nervous system, and the immune system
Placebo Effect: Genuine physiological improvement from expectation; involves endogenous opioid release (blocked by naloxone) and descending pain inhibitory pathways
Nocebo Effect: Harm or worsening of symptoms from negative expectations; involves CCK-mediated hyperalgesia
Pain Catastrophizing: Rumination, magnification, and helplessness about pain — the strongest psychological predictor of pain disability beyond tissue damage
Fear-Avoidance Model: Vlaeyen and Linton's model of chronic pain chronification — catastrophic appraisal → fear of movement → avoidance → deconditioning → disability
Sleep Stages: NREM (N1, N2, N3/slow-wave) and REM (rapid eye movements, vivid dreams, muscle atonia); 90-minute cycles; REM for emotional memory, slow-wave for declarative memory
REM Sleep: Characterized by high brain activity, muscle paralysis, vivid dreaming; critical for emotional regulation and memory consolidation
Circadian Rhythms: Approximately 24-hour biological cycles regulated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) and light exposure
Sleep Deprivation: Impairs cognitive function, immune function, emotional regulation, and increases accident risk
Psychopharmacology: The study of how drugs affect psychological functioning and behavior
SSRIs: Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors — most commonly prescribed antidepressants; block serotonin reuptake; first-line for depression and anxiety
Benzodiazepines: GABA-A agonists; effective short-term anxiolytics; risk of dependence
Antipsychotics: Typical (D2 blockers) and atypical (D2 + 5HT2A blockers); treat positive symptoms of schizophrenia
Mood Stabilizers: Lithium, valproate, lamotrigine; first-line treatments for bipolar disorder
Stimulants: Methylphenidate and amphetamines; first-line pharmacological treatment for ADHD
Ketamine: NMDA receptor antagonist with rapid antidepressant effects in treatment-resistant depression; challenges traditional monoamine hypothesis
ECT (Electroconvulsive Therapy): Electrically inducing seizures under general anesthesia; highly effective for severe, treatment-resistant depression
Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS): Non-invasive brain stimulation using magnetic fields; approved for treatment-resistant depression
Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS): Surgical implantation of electrodes to stimulate specific brain circuits; used for treatment-resistant OCD and depression
Evolutionary Psychology: Applies Darwinian evolutionary theory to explain psychological mechanisms as adaptations to ancestral environments (EEA)
Inclusive Fitness: Hamilton's concept that natural selection acts on genes shared with relatives, not just direct offspring
Reciprocal Altruism: Trivers's concept that cooperation evolves when benefits are exchanged between individuals over time
Parental Investment Theory: Trivers's theory that the sex investing more in offspring (typically female) will be more choosy in mate selection
Sexual Selection: Darwin's mechanism of selection through mate choice and competition; explains many sexually dimorphic traits
Preparedness Theory: Seligman's evolutionary account of phobias — humans are evolutionarily prepared to rapidly acquire fear of ancestrally dangerous stimuli (snakes, heights)
Conditioned Taste Aversion: Garcia effect — one-trial learning of food-illness association across hours delay; violates classical conditioning's contiguity principle; demonstrates biological preparedness
Forensic Psychology: Application of psychological knowledge to legal contexts — competency evaluations, eyewitness testimony, risk assessment, offender rehabilitation
Eyewitness Testimony: Vulnerable to misinformation effect, post-event suggestion, leading questions, stress, and time delay (Loftus)
Risk Assessment: Clinical evaluation of violence or reoffending risk; uses structured instruments (HCR-20, PCL-R) combining static and dynamic factors
AntiSocial Personality Disorder (ASPD): A pervasive pattern of disregard for others' rights; behavioral emphasis; different from psychopathy which additionally requires affective/interpersonal features
Psychopathy: Hare's PCL-R construct: Factor 1 (interpersonal/affective — grandiosity, callousness, shallow affect) + Factor 2 (antisocial lifestyle — impulsivity, criminal behavior)
Hare PCL-R: 20-item scale assessing psychopathy; Factor 1 predicts violent reoffending; approximately 15-25% with ASPD qualify as psychopaths
Dangerousness Assessment: The prediction of future violent behavior based on clinical, actuarial, and structured professional judgment approaches
Informed Consent (Clinical): Patients must be fully informed of purpose, risks, benefits, and alternatives before agreeing to treatment
Duty to Warn (Tarasoff): The legal and ethical obligation of therapists to warn identifiable potential victims when clients make credible threats
Confidentiality Limits: Confidentiality may be broken when there is serious, credible, imminent risk to the client or others
Mandatory Reporting: Legal obligation to report suspected child abuse or neglect to appropriate authorities
Positive Psychology: Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi's scientific movement studying human flourishing, strengths, and well-being
PERMA Model: Seligman's five pillars of well-being: Positive Emotions, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Achievement
Flow: Csikszentmihalyi's state of complete absorption in a challenging activity; requires skill-challenge balance, clear goals, and immediate feedback
Gratitude: Emmons and McCullough's research showing gratitude interventions (journals, letters) produce significant increases in well-being and reductions in depression
Character Strengths: VIA Classification of 24 character strengths organized under six virtues; applying signature strengths predicts well-being
Optimism: Scheier and Carver's generalized expectancy of good outcomes; predicts better health, coping, and well-being
Learned Optimism: Seligman's model of changing explanatory style — attributing bad events to external, unstable, specific causes rather than internal, stable, global ones
Hope Theory: Snyder's model — Hope = Agency thinking (will) + Pathway thinking (way); distinct from optimism; predicts academic and athletic achievement, coping with illness
Broaden-and-Build Theory: Fredrickson's theory that positive emotions broaden momentary thought-action repertoires and build lasting personal resources — contrasting with negative emotions' narrowing function
Subjective Well-Being (SWB): Diener's operational definition of happiness: Positive affect + absence of Negative affect + Life Satisfaction
Psychological Well-Being: Ryff's six-dimensional model: Autonomy, Environmental Mastery, Personal Growth, Positive Relations, Purpose in Life, Self-Acceptance
Hedonic Treadmill: Brickman and Campbell's concept that people adapt to positive and negative life changes, returning to a relatively stable happiness baseline
Self-Actualization: Maslow's concept of realizing one's full potential; the highest need in the hierarchy
Meaning in Life: Frankl's logotherapy — the primary human motivation; people can endure suffering if they can find meaning in it
Peak Experiences: Maslow's concept of moments of intense joy, wonder, and fulfillment associated with self-actualization
Neuropsychology: Studies relationships between brain structure/function and behavior/cognition; uses neuropsychological testing, neuroimaging, and lesion analysis
Trail Making Test: Measures cognitive flexibility (TMT-B — alternating numbers and letters) and processing speed (TMT-A)
Wisconsin Card Sorting Test: Measures concept formation and cognitive flexibility; frontal patients perseverate
Stroop Effect: Slower color naming when ink color and color word are incongruent — demonstrates automatic reading interfering with controlled color naming; clinical variant shows attentional bias
Verbal Fluency: Generating words by letter (phonemic) or category (semantic) — sensitive to frontal lobe damage
Iowa Gambling Task: Bechara and Damasio's neuropsychological test of decision-making under uncertainty; vmPFC patients fail to learn advantageous strategy; demonstrates somatic marker hypothesis
Somatic Marker Hypothesis: Damasio's proposal that vmPFC integrates bodily emotional signals with decision-making; damage produces poor real-world decisions despite intact IQ
Capgras Delusion: The fixed belief that a close person has been replaced by an identical impostor; explained by disconnection between intact face recognition and damaged emotional response system
Prosopagnosia: Inability to recognize faces; caused by damage to fusiform face area in inferior temporal cortex
Neglect Syndrome: Failure to attend to stimuli on the contralateral side of space; typically caused by right parietal damage
Aphasias: Language impairments from brain damage; Broca's aphasia (non-fluent speech, intact comprehension); Wernicke's aphasia (fluent but meaningless speech, impaired comprehension)
Executive Dysfunction: Impaired planning, inhibition, cognitive flexibility, and working memory from PFC damage; seen in ADHD, TBI, frontal dementia, schizophrenia
Developmental Psychology: The scientific study of systematic psychological changes across the lifespan in physical, cognitive, and socioemotional domains
Nature vs. Nurture: The question of whether psychological characteristics are primarily determined by genetic inheritance or environmental experiences; modern answer — always an interaction
Critical Period: A developmentally sensitive time window during which specific experiences must occur for normal development (language acquisition; imprinting)
Adolescent Brain Development: Reward sensitivity increases before impulse control fully matures (PFC not fully developed until ~25 years); explains increased risk-taking
EmergingAdulthood: Arnett's concept of a distinct developmental period (18-25 years) in developed societies — identity exploration, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, and possibilities
IndustrialOrganizational (I/O) Psychology: The application of psychological principles to the workplace — personnel selection, training, performance appraisal, motivation, leadership, organizational behavior
Leadership Styles: Transformational (inspiring through vision) vs. transactional (contingent reward and management by exception)
McGregor's Theory X vs. Theory Y: Theory X — employees inherently dislike work, need control; Theory Y — employees are self-directed and seek responsibility
Maslach's Burnout: Three-dimensional syndrome — emotional exhaustion, depersonalization/cynicism, reduced personal accomplishment; results from person-job mismatch in six areas
Groupthink Conditions: High cohesiveness, insulation, directive leadership, high stress, lack of systematic decision procedures
Organizational Behavior: The study of individual and group behavior in organizational settings
Cross-Cultural Psychology: Studies how cultural factors influence behavior, cognition, and development; challenges WEIRD assumptions in psychological research
Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions: Individualism-collectivism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation, indulgence vs. restraint
Color Psychology: The study of how colors influence perceptions, emotions, and behavior
Neuroimaging Methods: fMRI (blood oxygenation; spatial resolution), EEG (electrical activity; temporal resolution), PET (metabolic activity; radioactive tracers), TMS (causal disruption)
Default Mode Network (DMN): Brain regions more active during rest/mind-wandering (mPFC, PCC, angular gyrus); supports self-referential thought, autobiographical memory, and social cognition; hyperactive in depression (rumination)
Mirror Neurons: Neurons in macaques that fire both when performing and observing an action; human 'mirror system' proposed to underlie empathy and imitation though direct evidence in humans is limited
Emotional Contagion: Automatic, unconscious 'catching' of another's emotional state through mimicry; distinct from empathy (which maintains self-other distinction)
Interoception: The sense of the internal state of the body; processed via anterior insula; linked to emotional awareness, self-regulation, and psychopathology
Alexithymia: Sifneos's construct of difficulty identifying and describing feelings, poor emotional awareness, and externally oriented thinking; associated with psychosomatic disorders and reduced benefit from emotion-focused therapy
Emotional Granularity: Barrett's concept of the precision of emotional categorization; higher granularity predicts better emotion regulation and lower depression and anxiety
Epigenetics: Heritable changes in gene expression without DNA sequence changes; methylation and histone modification respond to environmental experiences, providing a mechanism for nature-nurture interaction
Neurogenesis: Formation of new neurons, primarily in the hippocampal dentate gyrus; promoted by exercise and antidepressants; suppressed by chronic stress; possibly involved in antidepressant mechanism
Synaptic Plasticity: Strengthening or weakening of synaptic connections through LTP (long-term potentiation) and LTD (long-term depression) — the cellular basis of learning and memory
Long-Term Potentiation (LTP): A persistent strengthening of synaptic connections based on recent activity; widely believed to be the neural basis of learning and memory
Neurotransmitter Reuptake: The process by which neurotransmitters are reabsorbed into the presynaptic neuron after release; target of many psychiatric medications
ActionPotential: An electrical impulse generated when a neuron's membrane potential reaches threshold, propagated along the axon by Na+/K+ exchange
Myelin Sheath: Fatty insulation around axons increasing signal transmission speed; damaged in multiple sclerosis
Synapse: The gap between neurons across which neurotransmitters carry signals
Neurodegeneration: Progressive loss of neurons; Alzheimer's (amyloid plaques, tau tangles, hippocampal degeneration), Parkinson's (substantia nigra dopamine neurons), Huntington's (striatum)
Alzheimer's Disease: The most common dementia; characterized by progressive memory loss (episodic first), personality changes, and cognitive decline; early hippocampal degeneration
Dementias: Progressive cognitive impairment affecting multiple domains; types include Alzheimer's, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal, and mixed
Comorbidity: The co-occurrence of two or more distinct disorders in one individual — extremely common; suggests shared underlying mechanisms; challenges categorical classification
Diathesis-Stress Model: Psychological disorders develop when a biological or psychological predisposition (diathesis) is activated by environmental stressors
Transdiagnostic Approach: Targeting shared processes underlying multiple disorders (emotional dysregulation, experiential avoidance, intolerance of uncertainty) rather than disorder-specific symptoms
HiTOP: Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology — dimensional, hierarchical alternative to DSM categorical approach; reflects comorbidity patterns and spectrum findings
RDoC: NIMH's Research Domain Criteria — dimensional, neurobiologically-anchored research framework cutting across DSM categories to identify mechanisms
DSM-5: American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual using categorical diagnoses with specific symptom criteria; updated to DSM-5-TR in 2022
ICD-11: WHO's International Classification of Diseases — the global diagnostic system; recognizes Complex PTSD, burnout as occupational phenomenon
Dimensional vs. Categorical: Ongoing debate about whether psychopathology is best conceptualized as distinct categories or as continuous dimensions that merge with normal functioning
Interpersonal Theory of Suicide: Joiner's model requiring thwarted belongingness + perceived burdensomeness (for desire) AND acquired capability (for action) to produce serious suicidal behavior
Safety Planning: A collaborative, individualized crisis intervention providing personalized steps for managing suicidal crises
Psychological First Aid (PFA): Evidence-informed early intervention after trauma focusing on safety, comfort, and connection — NOT trauma processing
Critical Incident Stress Debriefing (CISD): Single-session group trauma debriefing now discouraged by evidence showing it is ineffective and potentially harmful for some individuals
Trauma-Informed Care (TIC): An organizational and clinical approach recognizing the widespread impact of trauma; SAMHSA's six principles: Safety, Trustworthiness, Peer Support, Collaboration, Empowerment, Cultural Issues
Relapse Prevention (Marlatt): Model targeting high-risk situations, coping skills, and the Abstinence Violation Effect — catastrophic self-blaming after a lapse converting it into full relapse
Contingency Management: Behavioral intervention for substance use — providing tangible reinforcers for objectively verified abstinence; strongest evidence for stimulant use disorders
Motivational Enhancement Therapy (MET): Brief motivational therapy providing personalized assessment feedback; tested in Project MATCH; equivalent to longer therapies despite only four sessions
HarmReduction: A public health approach accepting that not all people will achieve abstinence; focusing on reducing the negative consequences of substance use
Addiction Neurobiology: Hijacking of the mesolimbic dopamine reward system; tolerance from neuroadaptation; opponent process producing dysphoria at baseline; incentive salience separating 'wanting' from 'liking'
Reward Prediction Error: Wolfram Schultz's discovery that dopamine neurons signal the difference between expected and actual reward — not reward itself; the neural basis of reinforcement learning
Cognitive Enhancers: Pharmacological and technological methods to improve cognitive performance in healthy individuals; raises neuroethical issues of fairness, coercion, and identity
Neuroethics: The ethics of neuroscience including cognitive enhancement, brain privacy, neuroscience in law, neural interventions, and the definition of personal identity
Free Will Debate: Libet's experiments showing readiness potential preceding conscious intention to move; raises philosophical questions about agency and moral responsibility
Conscious vs. Unconscious Processing: Evidence for extensive non-conscious processing from subliminal priming, blindsight, implicit memory, implicit attitudes, and LeDoux's fast fear pathway
Global Workspace Theory: Baars's theory that consciousness arises when information is 'broadcast' to a widely distributed neural workspace, making it available to multiple cognitive processes simultaneously
Hard Problem of Consciousness: Chalmers's formulation of the difficulty of explaining why physical brain processes give rise to subjective experience
Binding Problem: The neuroscience challenge of explaining how distributed parallel processing of different features is integrated into a unified conscious percept
Inattentional Blindness: Failure to notice unexpected stimuli when attention is engaged elsewhere — demonstrated by the 'invisible gorilla' experiment (Simons and Chabris)
Change Blindness: Failure to notice changes in a scene when the change occurs during a visual disruption
Cocktail Party Effect: The ability to selectively attend to one voice among many while still detecting personally significant information in the unattended channel
Attentional Networks: Posner and Petersen's three networks — Alerting (vigilance, noradrenergic), Orienting (directing attention, parietal-frontal), Executive Control (conflict resolution, ACC and lateral PFC)
Selective Attention: The ability to focus on one stimulus while filtering out others
Divided Attention: Attempting to process two or more streams of information simultaneously — impairs performance on demanding tasks
Embodied Cognition: The proposal that cognition is fundamentally shaped by the body's physical form and sensorimotor interactions — challenging purely computational mind models
Metacognition: Thinking about and regulating one's own cognitive processes; basis for metacognitive therapy (Wells) targeting beliefs about thinking rather than thought content
Cognitive Load Theory: Sweller's framework — working memory has limited capacity; effective instruction minimizes extraneous load, manages intrinsic load, and maximizes germane (schema-building) load
Dual Coding Theory: Paivio's theory that information encoded in both verbal and imagery systems is better remembered than information in only one code
Spaced Repetition: Reviewing material at increasing intervals produces superior long-term retention compared to massed practice — one of the most empirically supported study strategies
Retrieval Practice (Testing Effect): Actively recalling information from memory produces better long-term retention than passive review — more effective than re-reading
Interleaving: Mixing different types of problems or subjects during practice — produces better learning than blocked practice despite feeling more difficult
Desirable Difficulties: Bjork's concept that learning conditions that feel more difficult during study (spacing, interleaving, testing) produce better long-term retention
Cognitive Bias: A systematic pattern of deviation from rational judgment; includes confirmation bias, availability heuristic, representativeness heuristic, anchoring, framing effects, sunk cost fallacy
Confirmation Bias: The tendency to seek, interpret, and remember information that confirms pre-existing beliefs
Availability Heuristic: Judging probability by how easily examples come to mind — leads to overestimating the frequency of dramatic events
Representativeness Heuristic: Judging probability by similarity to a prototype — leads to base rate neglect and the conjunction fallacy
Anchoring: Disproportionate influence of initial information on subsequent judgments
Prospect Theory: Kahneman and Tversky's descriptive theory of decision-making under uncertainty — losses loom larger than equivalent gains (loss aversion ~2:1 ratio); reference points matter
Endowment Effect: The tendency to place greater value on objects one already owns than on equivalent unowned objects — explained by loss aversion
Sunk Cost Fallacy: Continuing to invest in a failing course of action because of past irrecoverable investments
Framing Effect: Decisions influenced by how options are presented (gain vs. loss framing) even when objectively identical
Decision Fatigue: Impaired decision quality and self-regulation after making many successive decisions
Systematic Review: A comprehensive, rigorous synthesis of all available evidence on a specific research question using explicit, reproducible methods
Effect Size: A quantitative measure of the magnitude of a phenomenon or treatment effect; essential for interpreting practical significance beyond statistical significance
Statistical Significance (p-value): The probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as observed if the null hypothesis were true; p < .05 is the conventional threshold (but does not measure effect size)
Power: The probability of detecting a true effect if it exists; determined by effect size, sample size, and alpha level; low power contributes to replication failure
Race and Intelligence: The debate about whether observed IQ differences between racial groups reflect genetic differences; scientific consensus — primarily explained by environmental, historical, and socioeconomic factors
SexDifferences in Psychology: Most psychological sex differences are small to negligible (Hyde's gender similarities hypothesis); those that exist (mental rotation, physical aggression) are partly culturally modifiable
Intersectionality: Kimberlé Crenshaw's framework recognizing that multiple social identities (race, gender, class) intersect to create unique experiences of privilege and oppression that cannot be understood separately
Psychological Safety: Edmondson's team climate concept where members feel safe to take interpersonal risks; the strongest predictor of team learning and performance in Google's Project Aristotle
Positive Organizational Psychology: Applying positive psychology principles to workplace flourishing — engagement, strengths-based management, psychological safety
Resilience in Organizations: The capacity of organizations to adapt successfully to disruption and adversity while maintaining core functioning
Cognitive Complexity: The degree of differentiation in one's construct system for understanding others — more dimensions, more nuanced social perception (Kelly's personal construct theory)
Self-Complexity: Linville's concept of having many distinct, non-overlapping self-aspects; high self-complexity buffers against mood swings — when one role-based self-aspect is threatened, others remain intact
Psychological Reactance: Brehm's theory — when behavioral freedoms are threatened, people are motivated to restore them, often by doing the opposite of what was demanded
Emotional Intelligence Applied to Therapy: Emotionally intelligent therapists recognize and use their own and clients' emotional signals as clinical data
Virtue Ethics in Psychology: The application of Aristotelian virtue ethics — focusing on character strengths and eudaimonia — to professional practice and ethical decision-making

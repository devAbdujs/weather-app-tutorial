Pharmacy is the science and technique of preparing, dispensing, and reviewing drugs and providing additional clinical services
 Pharmacist is a healthcare professional who practices in pharmacy
 Pharmacology is the branch of medicine and biology concerned with the study of drug action
 Pharmacokinetics is the study of drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion
 Pharmacodynamics is the study of the biochemical and physiological effects of drugs on the body
 Pharmaceutical care involves the responsible provision of drug therapy for the purpose of achieving definite outcomes that improve patients' quality of life.
Pharmaceutical manufacturing is the process of producing pharmaceutical drugs by industrial scale methods
 Compounding is the creation of a particular pharmaceutical product to fit the unique needs of a patient
 Extemporaneous compounding is the preparation of a pharmaceutical product from scratch according to a prescription or a doctor's order
 Quality control in pharmaceutical manufacturing ensures that drugs are manufactured consistently and meet quality standards
 Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) is a system for ensuring that products are consistently produced and controlled according to quality standards.
 Dispensing is the process of preparing and giving out prescribed medications
 Prescription is a written direction from a prescriber to a pharmacist for the compounding, dispensing, and administration of drugs to a patient
 Auxiliary labels provide additional instructions or warnings about how to use a medication
 Pharmaceutical calculations involve mathematical calculations related to medication dosages, dilutions, and compounding
 Pharmaceutical packaging is the process of enclosing pharmaceutical products for distribution, storage, sale, and use
 Institutional formulary is a list of medications approved for use within a healthcare institution.
 Community pharmacy is a pharmacy that deals directly with the public and provides prescription drugs, over-the-counter medications, and other health-related services
 Hospital pharmacy is a specialized field of pharmacy that focuses on the safe and effective use of medications in a hospital setting
 Clinical pharmacy is a branch of pharmacy where pharmacists provide patient care that optimizes the use of medication and promotes health, wellness, and disease prevention
 Pharmaceutical care is a patient-centered, outcomes-oriented pharmacy practice that requires the pharmacist to work in concert with the patient and the patient's other healthcare providers to promote health, prevent disease, and assess, monitor, initiate, and modify medication use to assure that drug therapy regimens are safe and effective.
Regulatory affairs in pharmacy involves ensuring compliance with laws, regulations, guidelines, and standards related to pharmaceuticals
 Drug scheduling is the classification of drugs into categories based on their potential for abuse, addiction, and medical use
 Drug approval process is the process by which pharmaceutical drugs are approved for sale and marketing by regulatory authorities.
Drug Information: Drug information involves providing accurate and unbiased information about medications to healthcare professionals and patients
 Drug interactions occur when one drug affects the activity of another drug when both are administered together
 Adverse drug reactions (ADR) are unwanted or harmful reactions experienced after the administration of a drug or combination of drugs under normal conditions of use.
Pharmacy Ethics: Pharmacy ethics encompasses the moral principles and values that guide the profession of pharmacy
 Patient confidentiality is the ethical duty of healthcare professionals to protect patient information
 Informed consent is the process by which a healthcare provider ensures that a patient understands the risks and benefits of a medical treatment or procedure before agreeing to it.
Pharmacy Economics:Pharmacy economics involves the financial aspects of pharmacy practice, including pricing, reimbursement, and cost-effectiveness
 Cost-benefit analysis is a method of comparing the costs and benefits of a decision, policy, or program to determine its feasibility or desirability
 Third-party payers are entities other than the patient and healthcare provider that reimburse healthcare expenses, such as insurance companies or government programs.
Pharmacy Management: Pharmacy management involves overseeing the operations of a pharmacy, including staffing, inventory management, and customer service
 Inventory management is the process of efficiently overseeing the constant flow of products into and out of an existing inventory
 Staffing involves recruiting, hiring, training, and managing employees within a pharmacy.
Pharmacy Technology:Pharmacy technology encompasses the tools, equipment, and systems used to support pharmacy operations
 Pharmacy automation refers to the use of technology to streamline and automate pharmacy processes, such as medication dispensing and inventory management
 Electronic health records (EHRs) are digital versions of patients' paper charts that contain their medical history, diagnoses, medications, treatment plans, immunization dates, allergies, radiology images, and laboratory test results.
Pharmacy law encompasses the laws, regulations, and legal principles that govern the practice of pharmacy
 Drug laws regulate the manufacture, distribution, dispensing, and use of medications
 Controlled substances laws regulate the production, distribution, and use of controlled substances
 Patient privacy laws protect patients' rights to privacy and confidentiality regarding their medical information.

Steady State: The condition in which the rate of drug administration equals the rate of drug elimination, typically reached after 4 to 5 half-lives of regular dosing
Prodrug: A pharmacologically inactive compound that is converted into an active drug through metabolic processes in the body, used to improve bioavailability or reduce toxicity
Volume of Distribution ($V_d$): A theoretical volume that relates the amount of drug in the body to the concentration of drug in the plasma, indicating the extent to which a drug distributes into extravascular tissues
Clearance ($Cl$): The volume of plasma from which a drug is completely removed per unit of time, representing the efficiency of drug elimination by the kidneys or liver
Therapeutic Window: The range of drug dosages or plasma concentrations which can treat disease effectively without having toxic effects; the area between the minimum effective concentration and the minimum toxic concentration
Michaelis-Menten Kinetics: A model of enzyme kinetics that describes how the rate of reaction depends on the concentration of the substrate, critical for understanding "saturation kinetics" where elimination becomes zero-order at high doses
Pharmacophore: The specific ensemble of steric and electronic features that is necessary to ensure the optimal supramolecular interactions with a specific biological target and to trigger its biological response
Enantiomers: Pairs of molecules that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other; in pharmacy, different enantiomers of the same drug can have vastly different therapeutic or toxic effects
Half-life ($t_{1/2}$): The time required for the amount or concentration of a drug in the body to decrease by exactly one-half, used to determine dosing intervals
Loading Dose: An initial higher dose of a drug given at the beginning of a treatment to reach the therapeutic steady-state concentration in the plasma more rapidly
Maintenance Dose: The regular dose of a drug required to maintain the desired therapeutic concentration at steady state by replacing the amount of drug eliminated between doses
Alligation Method: A mathematical technique used in pharmacy to calculate the proportions of two or more substances of different strengths needed to create a mixture of a desired intermediate strength
Isotonicity (E-value): The sodium chloride equivalent of a drug, representing the amount of NaCl that produces the same osmotic effect as 1 gram of the drug, used to adjust the osmotic pressure of ophthalmic and parenteral solutions
Thixotropy: A rheological property of certain pharmaceutical gels or fluids that become less viscous (flow more easily) when shaken and return to a gel-like state when left undisturbed, preventing sedimentation in suspensions
Noyes-Whitney Equation: A mathematical formula that describes the rate of dissolution of solid particles in a liquid, identifying factors like surface area and concentration gradient that influence drug absorption rates
Cellulose Acetate Phthalate (CAP): A common polymer used for enteric coating that remains insoluble at acidic stomach pH but dissolves at the higher pH of the small intestine to protect acid-labile drugs
P-glycoprotein (P-gp): An ATP-dependent efflux pump located in cell membranes that actively transports drugs out of cells (like the brain or gut), often acting as a major mechanism for multi-drug resistance and limited drug absorption
Potency vs. Efficacy: Potency refers to the concentration of drug required to produce a specific effect (position on the x-axis), while efficacy refers to the maximum effect a drug can produce regardless of dose (height on the y-axis)
Idiosyncratic Reaction: An unpredictable and abnormal response to a drug that is not dose-related and occurs in a small number of patients, often due to underlying genetic differences or metabolic abnormalities
Incompatibility: A physical or chemical interaction between two or more ingredients that leads to an undesirable product, such as precipitation, color change, or loss of therapeutic activity when drugs are mixed in the same syringe or IV bag

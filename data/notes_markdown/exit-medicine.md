Myocardial Infarction (MI): Irreversible necrosis of heart muscle secondary to prolonged ischemia, typically diagnosed via ST-elevation on ECG or elevated Cardiac Troponins. 
 Atrial Fibrillation (AFib): A supraventricular tachyarrhythmia characterized by uncoordinated atrial activation and ineffective atrial contraction; EKG shows absent P-waves and irregularly irregular R-R intervals. 
 Heart Failure (HF): A clinical syndrome where the heart cannot pump sufficient blood to meet metabolic demands, classified into HFrEF (reduced ejection fraction) and HFpEF (preserved). 
 Beck’s Triad: The clinical signs of cardiac tamponade: hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and jugular venous distention (JVD). 
Endocarditis: Infection of the endocardial surface of the heart, most commonly the valves; diagnosed using the Duke Criteria. 
 Aortic Dissection: A life-threatening tear in the inner layer of the aorta, often presenting with sudden tearing chest or back pain and asymmetric blood pressures. 
 Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM): An autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterized by asymmetric septal hypertrophy, the leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes. 
 Orthostatic Hypotension: A drop in systolic blood pressure of ≥20 mmHg or diastolic ≥10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing. 
 Mitral Stenosis: Narrowing of the mitral valve orifice, often caused by rheumatic heart disease, resulting in a mid-diastolic rumbling murmur. 
 Cardiac Output (CO): The volume of blood pumped by the heart per minute, calculated as Stroke Volume × Heart Rate.
Asthma: A chronic inflammatory airway disease characterized by reversible bronchoconstriction, airway hyperresponsiveness, and mucus production. 
 COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease): A persistent, progressive airflow limitation often associated with chronic bronchitis (productive cough) and emphysema (alveolar destruction). 
 Pneumothorax: The presence of air in the pleural space, leading to lung collapse; tension pneumothorax is a medical emergency requiring immediate needle decompression. 
 Pulmonary Embolism (PE): Obstruction of a pulmonary artery by a clot (usually from a DVT); CT Pulmonary Angiography is the diagnostic gold standard. 
Tuberculosis (TB): An infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, primarily affecting the lungs and characterized by caseating granulomas. 
 Sleep Apnea: A disorder where breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep, commonly associated with obesity and daytime somnolence. 
 Pleural Effusion: Abnormal accumulation of fluid in the pleural space; dullness to percussion and decreased breath sounds are hallmark physical signs. 
 Spirometry: The primary pulmonary function test used to measure the volume and speed of air inhaled and exhaled to diagnose obstructive vs. restrictive lung diseases. 
 Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS): Rapidly progressing respiratory failure characterized by diffuse alveolar damage and severe hypoxemia refractory to oxygen.

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD): Breaks in the lining of the stomach or duodenum, most commonly caused by Helicobacter pylori infection or chronic NSAID use. 
 Cirrhosis: Late-stage scarring (fibrosis) of the liver caused by many forms of liver diseases and conditions, such as hepatitis and chronic alcoholism. 
 Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD): Chronic symptoms or mucosal damage produced by the abnormal reflux of gastric contents into the esophagus. 
 Ascites: Pathologic accumulation of fluid within the peritoneal cavity, most commonly due to portal hypertension from cirrhosis. 
 Acute Pancreatitis: Inflammation of the pancreas, often triggered by gallstones or alcohol use; diagnosed by epigastric pain and elevated serum lipase (>3x normal). 
 Crohn's Disease: A type of Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) that can affect any part of the GI tract from mouth to anus, typically with skip lesions and transmural inflammation. 
 Ulcerative Colitis: An IBD characterized by continuous mucosal inflammation limited to the colon and rectum, often presenting with bloody diarrhea. 
 Cholecystitis: Inflammation of the gallbladder, usually due to a gallstone blocking the cystic duct; positive Murphy’s sign is highly suggestive. 
Mallory-Weiss Tear: A longitudinal mucosal tear at the gastroesophageal junction, typically caused by severe retching or vomiting. 
 Jaundice (Icterus): Yellowish discoloration of the skin and sclera caused by elevated serum bilirubin levels (>2.5 mg/dL). 
Diabetes Mellitus Type 1: Autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells leading to an absolute deficiency of insulin. 
 Diabetes Mellitus Type 2: A condition of relative insulin deficiency caused by peripheral insulin resistance and progressive beta-cell dysfunction. 
 DKA (Diabetic Ketoacidosis): A triad of hyperglycemia, ketonemia, and metabolic acidosis; an emergency complication primarily of Type 1 Diabetes. 
 Hyperthyroidism: Excess production of thyroid hormone, often due to Graves' Disease, presenting with tachycardia, weight loss, and heat intolerance. 
 Hypothyroidism: Deficient thyroid hormone production, often due to Hashimoto's Thyroiditis, presenting with bradycardia, weight gain, and cold intolerance. 
 Cushing’s Syndrome: Clinical state resulting from chronic exposure to excess glucocorticoids, presenting with moon facies and buffalo hump. 
 Addison’s Disease: Primary adrenal insufficiency where the adrenal glands do not produce enough cortisol or aldosterone; characterized by hyperpigmentation. 
 Acromegaly: A disorder caused by excessive growth hormone (GH) after the growth plates have closed, usually due to a pituitary adenoma. 
 Pheochromocytoma: A catecholamine-secreting tumor of the adrenal medulla that presents with paroxysmal hypertension, headaches, and palpitations. 
 Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c): A test that reflects average blood glucose levels over the preceding 3 months; ≥6.5% is diagnostic for diabetes. 
Acute Kidney Injury (AKI): A sudden decrease in kidney function defined by an increase in serum creatinine or a decrease in urine output. 
 Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD): Progressive, irreversible loss of renal function defined by GFR 3.5g/day), hypoalbuminemia, generalized edema, and hyperlipidemia. 
 Nephritic Syndrome: A syndrome characterized by glomerular inflammation, hematuria (coca-cola urine), hypertension, and mild-to-moderate proteinuria. 
 Pyelonephritis: An infection of the upper urinary tract and kidneys, typically presenting with fever, flank pain, and costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness. 
 Renal Calculi (Kidney Stones): Solid masses made of crystals (usually calcium oxalate) that cause severe colicky flank pain radiating to the groin. 
 BPH (Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia): Non-malignant enlargement of the prostate gland leading to obstructive lower urinary tract symptoms in aging men. 
 Dialysis: A process for removing waste and excess water from the blood when the kidneys can no longer perform these functions. 
 Hyperkalemia: Elevated serum potassium (>5.5 mEq/L); cardiac membrane stabilization with Calcium Gluconate is the first-line emergency treatment. 
 Hyponatremia: Low serum sodium (<135 mEq/L); rapid correction can lead to life-threatening Central Pontine Myelinolysis. 
APGAR Score: A quick assessment of a newborn's physical condition at 1 and 5 minutes after birth (Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, Respiration). 
 Croup (Laryngotracheobronchitis): A viral upper airway infection causing a characteristic barking cough, stridor, and hoarseness. 
 Intussusception: The telescoping of one segment of the intestine into another, presenting with currant jelly stools and a sausage-shaped mass. 
 Kawasaki Disease: An acute systemic vasculitis of unknown origin; the most feared complication is the development of coronary artery aneurysms. 
 Failure to Thrive (FTT): A condition where a child's weight or rate of weight gain is significantly below that of other children of similar age and sex. 
 Kernicterus: Permanent brain damage caused by excessively high levels of bilirubin (unconjugated) crossing the blood-brain barrier. 
 Bronchiolitis: Inflammation of the small airways (bronchioles) in infants, most commonly caused by Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV). 
 Tetralogy of Fallot: A congenital heart defect with four components: VSD, pulmonary stenosis, overriding aorta, and right ventricular hypertrophy. 
 Measles (Rubeola): A highly contagious viral infection characterized by high fever, cough, coryza, conjunctivitis, and Koplik spots. 
 Epiglottitis: A life-threatening bacterial infection of the epiglottis; classically presents with the tripod position and drooling. 
Preeclampsia: New-onset hypertension and proteinuria occurring after 20 weeks of gestation. 
 Eclampsia: The onset of tonic-clonic seizures in a woman with preeclampsia, necessitating magnesium sulfate for seizure management. 
 Ectopic Pregnancy: Implantation of a fertilized egg outside the uterine cavity, most commonly in the fallopian tube. 
 Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Blood loss ≥500 mL after vaginal delivery or ≥1000 mL after C-section; uterine atony is the most common cause. 
 Placenta Previa: Implantation of the placenta over or near the internal cervical os, presenting as painless vaginal bleeding in the third trimester. 
 Abruptio Placentae: Premature separation of a normally implanted placenta, presenting as painful vaginal bleeding and uterine tenderness. 
 Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS): A common hormonal disorder characterized by oligomenorrhea, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound. 
 Endometriosis: A condition where endometrial tissue grows outside the uterus, causing chronic pelvic pain and infertility. 
 Cervical Cancer: Malignancy of the cervix, largely caused by High-Risk Human Papillomavirus (HPV); Pap smears are the primary screening tool. 
 Menopause: The permanent cessation of menstruation, diagnosed retrospectively after 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea. 
Ischemic Stroke: A focal neurological deficit caused by blood flow obstruction to a part of the brain; Time is Brain emphasizes the urgency of thrombolysis. 
 Hemorrhagic Stroke: Intracranial bleeding that increases pressure on brain tissue; non-contrast CT is the first-line tool to rule this out. 
 Parkinson’s Disease: A neurodegenerative disorder characterized by resting tremors, bradykinesia, cogwheel rigidity, and postural instability. 
 Multiple Sclerosis (MS): An autoimmune demyelinating disease of the Central Nervous System (CNS), typically presenting with disseminated neurological symptoms over time. 
 Status Epilepticus: A seizure lasting more than 5 minutes or recurrent seizures without recovery of consciousness between episodes; a medical emergency. 
 Schizophrenia: A chronic mental disorder characterized by distortions in thinking (delusions), perception (hallucinations), and negative symptoms like flat affect. 
 Bipolar Disorder: A mood disorder characterized by alternating episodes of mania (elevated mood/energy) and depression. 
 Delirium: An acute, fluctuating disturbance in attention and awareness, often secondary to an underlying medical condition or drug toxicity. 
 Dementia: A chronic, progressive decline in cognitive function (e.g., Alzheimer’s) that interferes with daily living and independence. 
 Wernicke’s Encephalopathy: An acute neurological triad of confusion, ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia caused by Thiamine (B1) deficiency. 
Acute Abdomen: An emergency condition requiring urgent surgical evaluation, characterized by sudden, severe abdominal pain and signs of peritonitis. 
 Appendicitis: Inflammation of the appendix; McBurney’s point tenderness is the classic physical finding. 
 Septic Shock: Sepsis with circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities, requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg despite fluid resuscitation. 
 Anaphylaxis: A severe, systemic allergic reaction requiring immediate intramuscular Epinephrine. 
 Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS): A tool used to assess the level of consciousness based on Eye, Verbal, and Motor responses (Score 3 to 15). 
 Laparoscopy: A minimally invasive surgical technique using a camera and small incisions to perform abdominal procedures. 
 Informed Consent: The ethical and legal requirement to provide patients with sufficient information to make a decision about their treatment. 
 Triage: The process of prioritizing patients based on the severity of their condition to maximize survival in emergency settings. 
 Hemorrhoids: Swollen veins in the lower rectum and anus, often causing painless rectal bleeding during bowel movements. 
 Hernia: The protrusion of an organ or tissue through an abnormal opening in the muscle wall, such as inguinal or umbilical hernias.
Sepsis: Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, often identified by the qSOFA score (altered mental status, low BP, high respiratory rate). 
Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC): A systemic process characterized by both widespread microvascular thrombosis and profuse bleeding due to the consumption of clotting factors and platelets. 
Neutropenic Fever: A single oral temperature of $\geq$ 38.3°C or $\geq$ 38.0°C for 1 hour in a patient with an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 500 cells/$\mu$L; a medical emergency requiring immediate broad-spectrum antibiotics. 
Sickle Cell Crisis: Vaso-occlusive episodes in patients with Sickle Cell Disease where sickled RBCs obstruct capillaries, causing severe pain, organ ischemia, and potential acute chest syndrome. 
Infectious Mononucleosis: A viral syndrome caused primarily by Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV), characterized by the triad of fever, pharyngitis, and lymphadenopathy, often with atypical lymphocytosis. 
Multiple Myeloma: A plasma cell dyscrasia characterized by the "CRAB" features: Calcium elevation, Renal insufficiency, Anemia, and Bone lytic lesions. 
G6PD Deficiency: An X-linked recessive disorder where RBCs are susceptible to oxidative stress, leading to hemolytic anemia after exposure to certain drugs (e.g., primaquine), fava beans, or infection.

Murphy’s Sign: A clinical sign of acute cholecystitis where the patient experiences sharp pain and inspiratory arrest upon deep palpation of the right upper quadrant during inspiration. 
 Rovsing’s Sign: Pain felt in the right lower quadrant when the left lower quadrant is palpated; a classic sign of peritoneal irritation associated with appendicitis. 
 Esophageal Varices: Dilated submucosal veins in the lower third of the esophagus, usually due to portal hypertension, which carry a high risk of life-threatening hematemesis. 
 Hepatic Encephalopathy: A reversible syndrome of neurological and psychiatric dysfunction in patients with liver failure, caused by the accumulation of nitrogenous waste (ammonia) in the brain. 
 Achalasia: A primary esophageal motility disorder characterized by the failure of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) to relax and a lack of peristalsis; classically shows a bird's beak on barium swallow. 
Diverticulitis: Inflammation or infection of small pouches (diverticula) that develop along the walls of the intestines, typically presenting with left lower quadrant pain and fever. 
 Zenker’s Diverticulum: A herniation of the esophageal mucosa through the Killian's triangle (upper esophagus), leading to dysphagia, halitosis, and regurgitation of undigested food. 
Graves’ Disease: An autoimmune disorder and the most common cause of hyperthyroidism, characterized by the presence of TSH-receptor antibodies, proptosis (exophthalmos), and pretibial myxedema. 
 Thyroid Storm: A life-threatening hypermetabolic state caused by untreated or decompensated hyperthyroidism, presenting with high fever, tachycardia, agitation, and heart failure. 
 Myxedema Coma: A severe, life-threatening complication of hypothyroidism characterized by mental status changes, hypothermia, bradycardia, and non-pitting edema. 
 Pheochromocytoma: A catecholamine-secreting tumor of the adrenal medulla that presents with the classic triad of episodic headache, diaphoresis, and palpitations. 
 Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE): A chronic multisystem autoimmune disease characterized by the production of antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and symptoms like the malar (butterfly) rash and arthritis. 
 Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA): A chronic inflammatory disorder primarily affecting small joints symmetrically, characterized by synovial hyperplasia and the presence of Anti-CCP antibodies. 
 Gout: An inflammatory arthritis caused by the deposition of monosodium urate crystals in joints due to hyperuricemia; crystals appear needle-shaped and negatively birefringent. 
 Osteoarthritis (OA): A degenerative joint disease characterized by the breakdown of articular cartilage and the formation of osteophytes (bone spurs), usually worsening with activity.

Pyloric Stenosis: Hypertrophy of the pyloric sphincter in infants (usually 3–6 weeks old), presenting with forceful, projectile non-bilious vomiting and an olive-shaped palpable mass. 
 Ectopic Pregnancy: Implantation of a fertilized egg outside the uterine cavity, most commonly in the fallopian tube; a leading cause of maternal mortality in the first trimester. 
 Placental Abruption: Premature separation of the placenta from the uterine wall before delivery, typically presenting with painful vaginal bleeding and uterine tenderness. 
 HELLP Syndrome: A severe variant of preeclampsia characterized by Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, and Low Platelets. 
 Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (NRDS): Respiratory failure in preterm infants due to a deficiency of surfactant, leading to widespread alveolar collapse (atelectasis). 
Kernicterus: Permanent neurological damage caused by the deposition of unconjugated bilirubin in the basal ganglia of the brain in neonates with severe jaundice. 
 Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS): An endocrine disorder characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound; often associated with insulin resistance. 
Tension Pneumothorax: A life-threatening condition where air trapped in the pleural space causes a mediastinal shift, compressing the heart and great vessels; requires emergent needle decompression. 
 Cardiac Tamponade: Accumulation of fluid in the pericardial space resulting in reduced ventricular filling and hemodynamic compromise; characterized by pulsus paradoxus. 
 Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS): A standardized system used to assess the level of consciousness in trauma or neurological patients, scoring Eye (1-4), Verbal (1-5), and Motor (1-6) responses. 
 Aortic Dissection: A tear in the aortic intima allowing blood to flow into a "false lumen"; presents with sudden, "tearing" chest pain radiating to the back and asymmetric blood pressure in the arms. 
 Compartment Syndrome: Increased pressure within a muscle compartment that compromises circulation; characterized by the "6 Ps" (Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, Paralysis, Poikilothermia). 
 Anaphylaxis: A rapid-onset, systemic allergic reaction involving multiple organ systems; immediate treatment is intramuscular Epinephrine. 
 Warfarin: An oral anticoagulant that inhibits Vitamin K epoxide reductase, affecting factors II, VII, IX, and X; monitored using the International Normalized Ratio (INR). 
 Naloxone: A potent mu-opioid receptor antagonist used for the rapid reversal of opioid overdose and respiratory depression. 
Multiple Sclerosis (MS): A chronic autoimmune demyelinating disease of the central nervous system, often presenting with episodes of neurological deficits separated in time and space. 
 Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome: A spectrum of neurological disorders due to Thiamine (B1) deficiency; Wernicke's involves confusion and ataxia, while Korsakoff's involves permanent amnesia and confabulation. 
 Status Epilepticus: A seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes or consecutive seizures without a return to baseline consciousness; requires immediate treatment with benzodiazepines. 
 Delirium: An acute, fluctuating disturbance in attention and cognition, often triggered by underlying medical illness, medications, or metabolic imbalances. 
 Schizophrenia: A chronic psychiatric disorder characterized by "positive" symptoms (delusions, hallucinations) and negative symptoms (flat affect, social withdrawal). 
 Autonomy: The ethical principle that grants patients the right to self-determination and the ability to make informed decisions about their own medical treatment. 
 Beneficence: The ethical obligation of a healthcare provider to act in the best interest of the patient to promote their well-being. 
 Non-maleficence: The ethical principle of First, do no harm, requiring providers to avoid causing unnecessary pain or injury to patients.

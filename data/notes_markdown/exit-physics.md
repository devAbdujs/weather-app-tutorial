Newton's First Law of Motion: An object will remain at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by an external force. This is also known as the law of inertia.
Newton's Second Law of Motion: The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass. This is expressed as F = ma.
Newton's Third Law of Motion: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. This means that forces always occur in pairs.
Thermodynamics: The study of the relationships between heat, work, and energy. Key concepts include the laws of thermodynamics, which describe how energy is transferred and transformed.
Maxwell's Equations: A set of four fundamental equations that describe the behavior of electric and magnetic fields and how they interact with matter.
Wave-Particle Duality: The concept in quantum mechanics that every particle or quantum entity exhibits both wave and particle properties.
Schrodinger's Equation: A fundamental equation in quantum mechanics that describes how the quantum state of a physical system changes with time.
Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle: A principle in quantum mechanics stating that it is impossible to simultaneously know the exact position and momentum of a particle.
Ohm's Law: A fundamental law in electronics that states the current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points. This is expressed as V = IR.
Kirchhoff's Laws: Two rules for analyzing complex circuits: Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) states that the total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving, and Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) states that the total voltage around a closed loop is zero.
Electromagnetic Spectrum: The range of all types of electromagnetic radiation, including radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays.
Photoelectric Effect: The phenomenon where electrons are emitted from a material when it is exposed to light. This effect was explained by Albert Einstein using the concept of photons.
Special Relativity: A theory proposed by Albert Einstein that describes the physics of moving bodies at speeds close to the speed of light and introduces the concepts of time dilation and length contraction.
General Relativity: Einstein's theory of gravitation that describes gravity as a curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy.
Superconductivity: A phenomenon where a material exhibits zero electrical resistance and expulsion of magnetic fields below a certain temperature.
Nuclear Fission: The process of splitting a large atomic nucleus into smaller nuclei, releasing a large amount of energy.
Nuclear Fusion: The process of combining two light atomic nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. This is the process that powers the sun and other stars.
Entropy: A measure of the disorder or randomness of a system. In thermodynamics, it is a central concept in the second law of thermodynamics.
Quantum Entanglement: A phenomenon in quantum mechanics where particles become interconnected and the state of one particle instantaneously influences the state of another, regardless of the distance between them.
String Theory: A theoretical framework in which the point-like particles of particle physics are replaced by one-dimensional objects called strings. It aims to reconcile general relativity and quantum mechanics

Hooke's Law: The principle stating that the force needed to extend or compress a spring by some distance is proportional to that distance
Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion: Three laws describing the motion of planets around the Sun: (1) planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus, (2) a line segment joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time, and (3) the square of the orbital period of a planet is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit
Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction: A law stating that a change in magnetic flux through a loop of wire induces an electromotive force (EMF) or voltage in the wire
Gauss's Law: A law stating that the total electric flux through a closed surface is equal to the charge enclosed by the surface divided by the permittivity of free space
Coulomb's Law: A law describing the electrostatic force between electric charges. It states that the force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them
Doppler Effect: The change in frequency or wavelength of a wave in relation to an observer who is moving relative to the wave source
Lorentz Force: The force experienced by a charged particle in an electromagnetic field, arising from the combination of electric and magnetic forces
Beta Decay: A type of radioactive decay in which a beta particle (an electron or positron) is emitted from an atomic nucleus
Neutrino: A neutral subatomic particle with a tiny mass and no electric charge, rarely interacting with normal matter
Condensed Matter Physics: The study of the physical properties of condensed phases of matter, such as solids and liquids, including phenomena like superconductivity and magnetism
Quantum Tunneling: A quantum mechanical phenomenon where particles penetrate a potential barrier that would be classically insurmountable
Fermi Dirac Statistics: One of the two possible ways in which a system of indistinguishable particles may be distributed among a set of quantum states in thermal equilibrium
Bose Einstein Statistics: The statistical mechanics of identical particles with integer spin, applied especially to the study of the behavior of atomic systems at low temperatures
Positron: The antiparticle or antimatter counterpart of the electron, with the same mass as an electron and a positive electric charge
Raman Spectroscopy: A technique used to observe vibrational, rotational, and other low-frequency modes in a system by inelastic scattering of light
Boltzmann Constant: A physical constant relating energy at the individual particle level with temperature, equal to the gas constant divided by Avogadro's number
Geiger Counter: A device used to detect and measure ionizing radiation
Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR): A technique used in spectroscopy and imaging to study molecules, based on the magnetic properties of atomic nuclei
Lagrangian Mechanics: A reformulation of classical mechanics that uses the principle of least action and describes systems using coordinates and velocities, simplifying the analysis of complex constrained systems
Hamiltonian Mechanics: An evolution of the Lagrangian framework that uses coordinates and momenta as independent variables, providing the foundation for modern statistical mechanics and quantum theory
Standard Model: The theoretical framework in particle physics that describes the three fundamental forces—electromagnetic, weak, and strong nuclear—and the elementary particles that make up all matter
Higgs Boson: An elementary particle in the Standard Model that is the quantum excitation of the Higgs field, responsible for giving other particles their mass
Bernoulli's Principle: A principle in fluid dynamics stating that an increase in the speed of a fluid occurs simultaneously with a decrease in static pressure or a decrease in the fluid's potential energy
Navier-Stokes Equations: A set of partial differential equations that describe the motion of viscous fluid substances, representing the conservation of momentum and mass for Newtonian fluids
Carnot Efficiency: The maximum possible theoretical efficiency for a heat engine operating between two temperatures, given by the formula $\eta = 1 - (T_{cold} / T_{hot})$
Stefan-Boltzmann Law: A law stating that the total power radiated per unit surface area of a black body is directly proportional to the fourth power of its absolute temperature
Wien's Displacement Law: The principle that the wavelength of peak radiation from a black body is inversely proportional to its absolute temperature, explaining why hotter objects appear blue and cooler ones red
Planck's Law: Describes the spectral density of electromagnetic radiation emitted by a black body in thermal equilibrium at a given temperature, which resolved the 'ultraviolet catastrophe'
Compton Effect: The phenomenon where an X-ray or gamma ray photon scatters off an electron, resulting in a decrease in energy and an increase in wavelength, proving the particle nature of light
Pauli Exclusion Principle: A quantum mechanical principle stating that two or more identical fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state simultaneously within a quantum system
Fermi Energy: The energy of the highest occupied quantum state in a system of non-interacting fermions at absolute zero temperature, critical for understanding the conductivity of metals
Brillouin Zone: A uniquely defined primitive cell in reciprocal space used to describe the wave-related properties of a crystal lattice in solid-state physics
Meissner Effect: The complete expulsion of a magnetic field from the interior of a superconductor as it transitions into the superconducting state
Hall Effect: The production of a voltage difference across an electrical conductor, transverse to an electric current in the conductor and to an applied magnetic field perpendicular to the current
Lorentz Transformation: A set of linear transformations used to relate the space and time coordinates of two observers moving at a constant velocity relative to each other in special relativity
Schwarzschild Radius: The distance from the center of a non-rotating black hole to the event horizon, where the escape velocity equals the speed of light
Noether's Theorem: A fundamental theorem stating that every differentiable symmetry of the action of a physical system has a corresponding conservation law, such as time symmetry leading to energy conservation
Poynting Vector: A vector representing the directional energy flux (the rate of energy transfer per unit area) of an electromagnetic field, defined as the cross product of the electric and magnetic fields

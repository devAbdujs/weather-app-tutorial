Microeconomics examines individual decision-making units, such as consumers and firms, and their interactions in specific markets. 
Opportunity cost represents the value of the next best alternative that must be forgone when a choice is made. It highlights the trade-offs involved in decision-making.
 Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole, focusing on aggregate variables like GDP, unemployment, and inflation. 
 Supply and demand determine the price and quantity of goods and services in a market. 
 The law of demand states that, all else equal, as the price of a good increases, the quantity demanded decreases. 
 The law of supply states that, all else equal, as the price of a good increases, the quantity supplied increases. 
 Equilibrium is the point where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. 
 Price elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price. 
 Elastic goods have a high responsiveness to price changes, while inelastic goods have a low responsiveness. 
 Marginal utility is the additional satisfaction gained from consuming an additional unit of a good. 
 Diminishing marginal returns occur when adding an additional factor of production results in a smaller increase in output. 
 Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone when making a decision. 
 Comparative advantage occurs when a country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country. 
 Absolute advantage occurs when a country can produce more of a good with the same amount of resources as another country. 
 Fiscal policy involves government spending and taxation decisions to influence the economy. 
 Monetary policy involves controlling the money supply and interest rates to influence the economy. 
 Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services rises, eroding purchasing power. 
 Unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is jobless and actively seeking employment. 
 GDP (Gross Domestic Product) is the total value of all goods and services produced within a country in a given period. 
 Real GDP adjusts for inflation, while nominal GDP does not. 
 Economic growth is an increase in the capacity of an economy to produce goods and services, compared from one period of time to another. 
 Development economics studies the economic development process in low-income countries, focusing on improving living conditions. 
 Industrialization refers to the process of transforming an economy from primarily agricultural to one based on manufacturing. 
 Urbanization is the increasing concentration of population in cities and towns. 
 Trade policy encompasses the regulations and agreements that control imports and exports to foreign countries. 
 Balance of payments is the record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world. 
 Exchange rates determine how much one currency is worth in terms of another currency. 
 Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) is a theory that states that exchange rates should adjust so that identical goods cost the same in different countries. 
 Econometrics applies statistical and mathematical methods to economic data to test hypotheses and forecast future trends. 
 Economies of scale occur when increasing production leads to lower costs per unit due to factors like specialization and bulk buying. 
 Public goods are non-excludable and non-rivalrous, meaning that one person's use of the good does not reduce its availability to others, and people cannot be prevented from using it. 
 Market failure occurs when the allocation of goods and services is not efficient, leading to a net social welfare loss. 
 Externalities are costs or benefits of economic activities that are not reflected in market prices and affect third parties. 
 Monopoly exists when a single firm controls the entire market for a good or service, limiting competition. 
 Oligopoly is a market structure characterized by a small number of firms that have significant market power. 
 Perfect competition is a market structure where many firms offer identical products, and no single firm can influence the market price. 
 Consumer surplus is the difference between what consumers are willing to pay for a good and what they actually pay. 
 Producer surplus is the difference between what producers are willing to accept for a good and what they actually receive.

Efficiency Wage Theory: The idea that it may benefit firms to pay workers a wage higher than the market-clearing level to increase productivity, reduce turnover, and attract higher-quality employees

The Impossible Trinity (Trilemma): A concept in international economics stating that it is impossible for a country to simultaneously have a fixed foreign exchange rate, free capital movement, and an independent monetary policy

Pigouvian Tax: A tax levied on any market activity that generates negative externalities (costs not included in the market price), intended to correct an inefficient market outcome by making the private cost equal to the social cost
 Coase Theorem: Suggests that if property rights are well-defined and transaction costs are zero, private parties can bargain to solve the problem of externalities efficiently, regardless of who holds the property rights

Say's Law: The classical economic principle stating that 'supply creates its own demand,' implying that production is the source of demand and that general overproduction (gluts) cannot exist in a free market
Creative Destruction: A term coined by Joseph Schumpeter describing the process of industrial mutation that incessantly revolutionizes the economic structure from within, destroying the old one and incessantly creating a new one through innovation
Secular Stagnation: A condition of negligible or no economic growth in a market-based economy, characterized by an excess of desired savings over desired investment, leading to persistent low interest rates and low growth
